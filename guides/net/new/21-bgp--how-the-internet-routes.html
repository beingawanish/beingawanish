Module 21 — BGP & How the Internet Routes

Networking Mastery Guide · Module 21 of 23

1. Why it matters

On 4 October 2021, Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp vanished from the internet for about six hours. Their servers were running. Their data centres had power. What disappeared was a handful of BGP announcements. A maintenance command took down Facebook's backbone, and its authoritative DNS servers, no longer able to reach the data centres behind them, did what they were built to do: they withdrew the BGP routes for their own anycast addresses. Within a couple of minutes no network on earth had a route to Facebook's DNS. Resolvers everywhere returned SERVFAIL, and retries from billions of apps piled extra load onto everyone else's resolvers.

That is the thing to hold onto about BGP. Every other module in this guide assumes that a packet addressed to a public IP will find its way there. BGP is the reason that assumption is true, and also the reason it sometimes stops being true for an hour at a time.

Module 9 gave you the one-line version: BGP is the path-vector protocol that runs between autonomous systems and optimises for policy rather than distance. This module opens it up. You will see what an AS is and why some networks pay others, how two routers bring up a session and what bytes they exchange, how a router picks one path out of many, why a /24 beats a /22 everywhere on earth, and how a mistake in one small network becomes a global outage.

It also matters closer to home than you might expect. In 2026, BGP is not only the protocol between ISPs. Your cloud provider runs it on Direct Connect, ExpressRoute and Cloud Router sessions. Large data centres use it as their only routing protocol. Your Kubernetes cluster may well be running it right now: Calico, MetalLB and Cilium all speak BGP to announce pod and service addresses to the physical network. When a LoadBalancer service stops answering from outside the cluster, the root cause is often a BGP session you didn't know existed.

2. The mental model

Start with the right picture of the internet. It is not one network. It is about 75,000 to 80,000 independently run networks, called autonomous systems (ASes), each with its own routers, its own policies and its own commercial interests. An AS is a network under one administration with one routing policy. Comcast is an AS. So is Cloudflare, a university, a mid-sized hosting company, and a bank that wants its own address space reachable over two ISPs.

No central authority tells these networks how to reach each other. Instead, each AS tells its neighbours which address blocks (prefixes) it can deliver traffic to. Those neighbours tell their neighbours, adding their own AS number to the front of a list as they go. That list is the AS_PATH. By the time a route for 203.0.113.0/24 reaches you, it reads something like 64497 64502 64511: "send it to 64497, who will hand it to 64502, who will hand it to 64511, which owns the prefix."

So a BGP route is a promise passed along a chain. Each link in the chain says, "I can get traffic there, and here is the chain of networks it will cross." This is why BGP is called path-vector. A distance-vector protocol like RIP only passes on a number. BGP passes on the whole path, and that path does two jobs at once. It prevents loops, because a router that sees its own AS number in a path rejects the route. And it gives each network something to make policy decisions on.

Here is the part that trips people up. BGP does not find the shortest path. It finds the path each network prefers, and preference is mostly about money. A network would rather send traffic to a customer (who pays it) than to a peer (free) than to a transit provider (who it pays). Path length is a tie-breaker that only counts after policy has had its say. The internet's routing is the sum of tens of thousands of selfish local decisions. That it converges on working paths at all is a result of a few conventions everyone follows. That it sometimes doesn't is a result of someone not following them.

Three properties fall out of this picture, and you will use them all through the module:

  • You control your outbound traffic; you only influence your inbound traffic. Your routers choose where your packets leave. Other networks' routers choose how packets reach you, using their policies, not yours.
  • BGP trusts what it hears. The base protocol has no way to check that the AS announcing a prefix is allowed to. Every hijack and leak in this module comes from that one gap. RPKI is the partial patch.
  • Paths are asymmetric. The route from A to B and the route from B to A are chosen by different networks with different policies. They rarely match.

3. How it works

3.1 Autonomous systems and AS numbers

Each AS gets an AS number (ASN) from a regional internet registry (ARIN, RIPE NCC, APNIC, LACNIC, AFRINIC). ASNs were 16 bits until RFC 4893 (now RFC 6793) widened them to 32 bits. The ranges you need to recognise:

Range What it is Standard
1 – 64495 Public 16-bit ASNs RFC 1930 / IANA registry
23456 AS_TRANS: placeholder a 4-byte ASN shows to a 2-byte-only peer RFC 6793
64496 – 64511 Documentation and examples (used throughout this module) RFC 5398
64512 – 65534 Private use, like RFC 1918 for ASNs RFC 6996
65536 – 65551 Documentation, 32-bit RFC 5398
131072 – 4199999999 Public 32-bit ASNs (allocated in blocks) IANA registry
4200000000 – 4294967294 Private use, 32-bit RFC 6996

Private ASNs matter more than they look. Data-centre fabrics and Kubernetes clusters use them by the hundred (§Deep dive: 2026 context), and they must be stripped before a route reaches the public internet.

Some real ASNs you will see in looking glasses: AS13335 (Cloudflare), AS15169 (Google), AS16509 (Amazon), AS32934 (Facebook/Meta), AS3356 (Lumen), AS1299 (Arelion), AS174 (Cogent), AS2914 (NTT).

3.2 Transit, peering and the money

Every BGP relationship between two ASes is one of two commercial kinds.

Transit is a paid service. The customer pays the provider, and the provider promises to carry the customer's traffic to the whole internet and to announce the customer's prefixes to the whole internet. The customer usually receives either a full table (every route the provider knows) or just a default route.

Peering is an exchange between equals. Two networks agree to swap traffic for each other's customers, usually without money changing hands ("settlement-free"). Crucially, a peer only gives you routes to its own and its customers' prefixes. It is not selling you the rest of the internet.

A handful of networks buy transit from nobody. They reach every destination through their own customers and through settlement-free peering with each other. These are called Tier 1 networks; Lumen (AS3356), Arelion (AS1299), NTT (AS2914) and Cogent (AS174) are commonly listed. "Tier 1" is a convention, not a certification, and the exact membership is argued about.

The commercial relationships translate directly into routing policy. The standard model is named after Lixin Gao and Jennifer Rexford, who showed in 2001 that if every network follows these rules, BGP is guaranteed to converge:

The export rules produce what's called valley-free routing. A path goes up through providers, optionally across one peering link, then down through customers. It never goes down and back up. A path that dips into a network and climbs out again (provider → you → another provider) would mean you are giving free transit between two networks bigger than you. When that happens by accident, it is a route leak (RFC 7908 has the formal taxonomy).

3.3 Internet exchange points

Peering needs a place to connect. You can run a private cable between two routers in the same building (a private network interconnect, PNI). Or you can connect once to an internet exchange point (IXP) and peer with hundreds of networks over a single port.

An IXP is, physically, a big Ethernet switch fabric in one or more data centres. Every member gets a port and an address on a shared peering LAN. DE-CIX Frankfurt, AMS-IX and LINX are among the largest, with peak traffic in the tens of terabits per second. The IXP itself usually does not route anything. Members run BGP sessions directly with each other across the shared LAN.

Running hundreds of bilateral sessions is tedious, so most IXPs offer route servers (RFC 7947). A member opens one session to the route server, which collects everyone's routes, filters them (many route servers now drop RPKI-invalid routes) and reflects them out. Unlike a normal BGP speaker, a route server does not add its own ASN to the AS_PATH, and it leaves the NEXT_HOP pointing at the member who announced the route. Traffic flows member-to-member across the switch; the route server only carries the control plane. Route servers commonly run BIRD or OpenBGPD.

PeeringDB (peeringdb.com) is the public directory of who is present at which IXP and facility, and what their peering policy is. It is the first place a network engineer looks when planning where to peer.

3.4 eBGP and iBGP

BGP runs in two modes that share one protocol but behave differently.

eBGP (external) runs between routers in different ASes. It is the mode everything above has described. An eBGP speaker prepends its own ASN to the AS_PATH when it sends a route, and it sets NEXT_HOP to its own address. By default, eBGP packets are sent with IP TTL 1, because the neighbour is expected to sit on a directly connected link. (ebgp-multihop raises it; the GTSM trick in RFC 5082 inverts it and sends TTL 255, so the receiver can drop anything with TTL below 255 as spoofed from afar.)

iBGP (internal) runs between routers in the same AS. Its job is to carry external routes across your network, so that a router in London knows what your router in Frankfurt learned from a transit provider there. Three rules make iBGP different:

  1. No AS_PATH prepend. The route stays inside one AS, so the path doesn't change.
  2. NEXT_HOP is passed through unchanged. A route learned over eBGP in Frankfurt still names the Frankfurt transit router's address as next hop when it reaches London. If London has no IGP route to that address, the BGP route is unusable. This is why border routers almost always set next-hop-self on their iBGP sessions.
  3. iBGP split horizon. A route learned from one iBGP peer is never passed to another iBGP peer. Without an AS_PATH change, iBGP has no loop detection, so this rule stands in for it.

Rule 3 means every iBGP router must peer with every other one: a full mesh of n(n−1)/2 sessions. Fifty routers need 1,225 sessions. Two standard fixes exist. Route reflectors (RFC 4456) are allowed to break the split-horizon rule for their clients; they add ORIGINATOR_ID and CLUSTER_LIST attributes to stop loops. Confederations (RFC 5065) split one AS into private sub-ASes that speak a modified eBGP to each other. Route reflectors won in practice.

iBGP carries the external routes. An IGP (OSPF or IS-IS) still runs underneath to carry the routes to loopbacks and links inside the AS, including the next-hop addresses BGP depends on.

3.5 Sessions: TCP port 179 and a six-state machine

A BGP session is one ordinary TCP connection to port 179. BGP has no discovery and no multicast hellos, unlike OSPF. You configure each neighbour by address and ASN, and the routers connect. Running over TCP is a deliberate choice: BGP gets reliable, ordered delivery for free, so it never has to retransmit or sequence its own messages. Once a route is sent, it stays valid until it is explicitly withdrawn or the session dies.

Each session runs a finite state machine defined in RFC 4271 §8:

State What the router is doing
Idle Refusing connections. Starts here, and returns here after any error.
Connect Waiting for its outgoing TCP connection to complete.
Active Listening for the peer to connect in (despite the name, this is the passive state).
OpenSent TCP is up; it has sent OPEN and is waiting for the peer's OPEN.
OpenConfirm OPENs exchanged and accepted; waiting for the first KEEPALIVE.
Established Exchanging UPDATEs. The only state in which routes flow.

A session stuck flipping between Active and Connect means TCP itself isn't working: wrong address, a firewall dropping 179, a missing route to the neighbour, or a TTL problem. A session that reaches OpenSent and falls back to Idle means the OPEN was rejected, usually because of a wrong ASN or a mismatched capability. Those two cases cover most "BGP won't come up" tickets.

Two timers keep the session alive. The hold time is negotiated in the OPEN messages; the lower of the two offers wins. RFC 4271 suggests 90 seconds. Each side then sends a KEEPALIVE every hold/3 seconds, if it has nothing else to send. If no message arrives within the hold time, the router declares the peer dead, sends a NOTIFICATION, tears down the session and withdraws every route learned from it. Defaults vary: Cisco IOS uses 60/180 seconds keepalive/hold, FRR's datacenter profile uses 3/9 seconds.

3.6 Four messages (and a fifth)

Every BGP message starts with the same 19-byte header: a 16-byte marker of all ones, a 2-byte length and a 1-byte type. The maximum message size is 4,096 bytes, unless both sides negotiate extended messages (RFC 8654, up to 65,535).

Type Name Purpose
1 OPEN First message after TCP comes up: version, ASN, hold time, router ID, capabilities
2 UPDATE Announce new routes, withdraw old ones, or both
3 NOTIFICATION Report a fatal error. The session is closed right after it is sent.
4 KEEPALIVE Header only, 19 bytes. "I'm still here."
5 ROUTE-REFRESH Ask the peer to resend all its routes (RFC 2918), so new import policy can apply without a reset

Capabilities (RFC 5492) ride inside the OPEN and are how BGP has grown without a version 5. The important ones are multiprotocol extensions (RFC 4760, which is how BGP carries IPv6, VPN routes and EVPN), 4-octet ASNs, route refresh, graceful restart (RFC 4724) and add-path (RFC 7911). If a capability is not advertised by both sides, it is not used.

3.7 Path attributes

An UPDATE carries a set of path attributes that apply to every prefix in that UPDATE. The attributes are the raw material of every BGP decision. Here are the ones that matter:

Code Attribute Category What it carries
1 ORIGIN well-known mandatory How the route entered BGP: IGP (0), EGP (1), INCOMPLETE (2, usually redistributed)
2 AS_PATH well-known mandatory The ASes the route has crossed, newest first
3 NEXT_HOP well-known mandatory The IPv4 address to forward traffic to
4 MULTI_EXIT_DISC (MED) optional non-transitive A hint to a neighbouring AS about which of several links to use. Lower is better.
5 LOCAL_PREF well-known, iBGP only Your own AS's preference for this path. Higher is better. Never sent over eBGP.
8 COMMUNITIES optional transitive 32-bit tags (ASN:value) that carry policy signals (RFC 1997)
9, 10 ORIGINATOR_ID, CLUSTER_LIST optional non-transitive Loop prevention for route reflectors (RFC 4456)
14, 15 MP_REACH_NLRI, MP_UNREACH_NLRI optional non-transitive Announce and withdraw IPv6 and other address families (RFC 4760)
32 LARGE_COMMUNITY optional transitive 96-bit tags for 4-byte ASNs (RFC 8092)

"Transitive" means a router that doesn't understand the attribute still passes it on. That is how communities can travel several ASes.

Communities are the most useful attribute nobody teaches. They are how networks talk policy to each other. A transit provider might publish a list like this: tag your route 64497:70 and we will set our LOCAL_PREF to 70 (making it a backup); tag it 64497:3003 and we will prepend three times towards Europe. A few communities are standardised: NO_EXPORT (65535:65281) keeps a route inside the receiving AS, BLACKHOLE (65535:666, RFC 7999) asks the neighbour to drop traffic to the prefix during a DDoS, and GRACEFUL_SHUTDOWN (65535:0, RFC 8326) asks the neighbour to depreference a path before you take it down for maintenance.

3.8 Best-path selection: the order that decides everything

A router often learns the same prefix from several neighbours. It installs exactly one of them as best (or several, if multipath is configured and they tie far enough down the list). It decides by walking a fixed list of tie-breakers, stopping at the first one that separates the candidates. RFC 4271 §9.1 defines the core; vendors add steps. This is the order most implementations share, with vendor-specific steps marked:

Step Rule Prefer Notes
0 NEXT_HOP must be reachable — An unreachable next hop makes the path ineligible, not just worse
1 Weight Highest Cisco-only, local to one router, never sent
2 LOCAL_PREF Highest Default 100. Your AS-wide policy knob.
3 Locally originated Local Routes from network, aggregate or redistribution on this router
4 AS_PATH length Shortest An AS_SET counts as 1. Prepending makes a path look longer.
5 ORIGIN IGP < EGP < INCOMPLETE
6 MED Lowest Only compared between paths from the same neighbouring AS, unless always-compare-med
7 eBGP over iBGP eBGP Get traffic out of your network as soon as possible
8 IGP cost to NEXT_HOP Lowest "Hot-potato" routing: use the nearest exit
9 Oldest eBGP path Oldest Cisco; reduces churn. Many implementations skip it.
10 Router ID (ORIGINATOR_ID if reflected) Lowest
11 CLUSTER_LIST length Shortest RFC 4456
12 Neighbour address Lowest Final, deterministic tie-break

Read the list as a story. Steps 2 and 3 are policy: what you decided. Step 4 is what the rest of the internet is telling you about distance, in AS hops rather than milliseconds. Steps 5 and 6 are hints from neighbours. Steps 7 and 8 are about getting the packet off your network cheaply. Everything after that is a coin toss made deterministic.

Two consequences come up in every senior interview. First, LOCAL_PREF beats AS_PATH length. A customer route through four ASes beats a transit route through two, because step 2 runs before step 4. Second, AS_PATH length counts ASes, not latency. A one-AS path that crosses an ocean beats a two-AS path inside your city. BGP has no idea how long anything takes.

You will build this selector in Python in §9.

3.9 Prefix length, more-specifics and aggregation

BGP picks a best path per prefix. Forwarding then uses longest-prefix match across all prefixes (Module 9). These are two separate steps, and the interaction between them is the most powerful lever in all of internet routing.

Suppose AS 64511 announces 203.0.112.0/22. Someone else announces 203.0.113.0/24. They are different prefixes, so BGP never compares them. Both get installed. A packet to 203.0.113.10 matches both, and the /24 wins because it is longer. No LOCAL_PREF, no AS_PATH length, no policy of any kind can make a router prefer the /22 for that address. The more-specific always wins, everywhere it is accepted.

That fact is used deliberately every day. Operators announce more-specifics for traffic engineering (send this /24 only via Frankfurt), and DDoS mitigation services announce a customer's /24 to pull its traffic through a scrubbing centre. It is also how the worst hijacks work, as §Deep dive shows.

The global table would be unmanageable if anyone could announce anything, so the operational community enforces limits by convention. Most networks drop IPv4 prefixes longer than /24 and IPv6 prefixes longer than /48. No RFC mandates these numbers. They are what everyone filters on, so a /25 announced to the internet usually reaches almost nobody. Aggregation is the opposite force: announcing one /22 instead of four /24s keeps everyone's tables smaller. The global IPv4 table holds roughly 1 million prefixes in 2025–26 and IPv6 a little over 200,000. The precise count changes daily; bgp.potaroo.net tracks it.

3.10 Anycast

Module 13 introduced anycast as "the same IP announced from many PoPs." Now you can see the mechanism exactly. A CDN announces 198.51.100.0/24 from 300 locations, each with the same origin ASN. Every network on the internet receives several paths to that prefix and runs best-path selection as usual. Each picks one, usually the one with the highest LOCAL_PREF and then the shortest AS_PATH. Traffic from that network goes to whichever PoP sits at the end of the chosen path.

So "nearest" in anycast means nearest by BGP policy, not by geography or latency. A user in Lisbon whose ISP buys transit only from a carrier that peers with the CDN in Madrid will land in Madrid even if there is a PoP in Lisbon. CDNs spend real engineering effort on peering placement for exactly this reason.

Anycast fails over by withdrawal. If a PoP stops announcing, routers that used it fall back to their next-best path, typically within seconds to a few minutes. This is the same mechanism that took Facebook offline: its DNS servers withdrew their anycast routes when they decided they were unhealthy, and they all decided it at once. The DNS root servers (13 identities, well over a thousand instances) and public resolvers like 1.1.1.1 and 8.8.8.8 are all anycast. RFC 4786 is the operational guide.

3.11 Convergence

When something changes, BGP has to tell everyone, one hop at a time. How long the internet takes to agree again is called convergence, and for BGP it is slow by design.

An announcement of a new prefix spreads fast: each router runs best-path, sees a new route, passes it on. Seconds to tens of seconds globally.

A withdrawal is worse. When a prefix disappears, a router that loses its best path falls back to its next-best path, which may also be about to disappear but hasn't been withdrawn yet. It announces that path, then withdraws it, then tries a longer one. This is called path hunting (or path exploration). Measurements going back to Labovitz and colleagues in 2000 show withdrawals taking minutes to settle, where announcements take seconds.

Two mechanisms slow things down on purpose. The MinRouteAdvertisementInterval (MRAI) stops a router sending updates for the same destination to a peer more often than every 30 seconds on eBGP and 5 seconds on iBGP, as RFC 4271 suggests. It batches churn at the cost of delay. Implementations differ here, and data-centre profiles (FRR's datacenter, for one) set it to 0. Route flap damping (RFC 2439) suppresses prefixes that flap repeatedly. Early aggressive damping settings made outages worse, and RFC 7196 raised the thresholds.

Failure detection is the other half. A hold timer of 90 seconds means a silently dead peer (a cut fibre behind a media converter, say) can blackhole traffic for a minute and a half. Operators pair BGP with BFD (RFC 5880), which sends lightweight probes every few hundred milliseconds and tells BGP to drop the session in under a second.

3.12 Leaks, hijacks and RPKI, in one page

Because BGP trusts what it hears, two kinds of bad route spread:

  • A hijack is an announcement of a prefix by an AS that has no right to originate it. Sometimes it is a typo, sometimes malicious.
  • A route leak is a legitimate route passed somewhere the export rules say it should not go, typically a provider's or peer's routes re-announced to another provider. The origin is correct; the path is not.

Both are worst when they involve more-specifics, because §3.9's rule means the bad route wins outright wherever it is accepted.

The main defence deployed today is RPKI (Resource Public Key Infrastructure, RFC 6480) with Route Origin Validation (ROV, RFC 6811). The holder of an address block signs a Route Origin Authorization (ROA, RFC 6482, now RFC 9582) through its regional registry: "AS 64511 may originate 203.0.112.0/22, with prefixes up to length /24." Validators such as Routinator, rpki-client and FORT download and verify every ROA in the world and feed the result to routers over the RPKI-to-Router protocol (RFC 8210). Each route then gets one of three states:

ROV state Meaning Typical action
Valid A ROA covers the prefix, the origin AS matches, and the length is within maxLength Accept
Invalid A ROA covers the prefix, but the origin or the length doesn't match Drop
NotFound No ROA covers the prefix at all Accept. A shrinking share of the table as ROA coverage grows.

ROV checks the origin only. It cannot stop a leak, where the origin is correct, and it cannot stop an attacker who forges the path by putting the legitimate origin ASN at the end. Defences for those are newer: RFC 9234 adds BGP Roles and the Only-to-Customer (OTC) attribute to catch leaks, and ASPA (Autonomous System Provider Authorization) lets an AS publish its list of upstream providers so paths can be checked for valleys. ASPA was still being standardised and only beginning deployment in 2026. BGPsec (RFC 8205), which signs every hop, exists on paper and almost nowhere else.

5. A realistic example

You run network engineering for a SaaS company, AS 64496. You own 203.0.112.0/22 and have a ROA for it with maxLength /24. You buy 10 Gbit/s of transit from AS 64497 in Frankfurt and 10 Gbit/s from AS 64498 in Ashburn, Virginia. The plan was simple: Europeans arrive via Frankfurt, Americans via Ashburn.

On Monday the Ashburn port is at 9.2 Gbit/s and Frankfurt is at 1.1. Latency dashboards show European users paying an extra 80 ms. Here is how you work it.

Step 1: confirm what you are announcing. On your Frankfurt edge router (FRR here; output illustrative):

edge-fra# show bgp ipv4 unicast neighbors 192.0.2.1 advertised-routes
   Network          Next Hop            Metric LocPrf Weight Path
*> 203.0.112.0/22   0.0.0.0                  0         32768 i

Total number of prefixes 1

One prefix, no prepend, origin IGP. The Ashburn router shows the same. So your announcements are symmetric. The asymmetry is in how other networks choose between them.

Step 2: look from the outside. You can't see other networks' decisions from your own routers. A looking glass or a route collector can. RIPE RIS and RouteViews collect full tables from hundreds of networks; bgp.tools and RIPEstat put a web UI on top. Here is what a European eyeball network sees (illustrative):

lg> show ip bgp 203.0.112.0/22
BGP routing table entry for 203.0.112.0/22
Paths: (2 available, best #2)
  64505 64497 64496                         <- via a Tier 1 that buys nothing, then your Frankfurt transit
    Origin IGP, localpref 100, valid, external
  64506 64498 64496                         <- via 64506, which buys transit from your Ashburn provider
    Origin IGP, localpref 200, valid, external, best

The second path wins, and it wins at LOCAL_PREF. This European ISP buys transit from 64506 and gives that upstream LOCAL_PREF 200, perhaps because it is cheaper. 64506 in turn is a customer of your Ashburn transit, 64498, so 64506 learns your prefix as a customer route via 64498 and prefers it. Both paths are three ASes long. Policy, set two networks away from you, decided the outcome at step 2. Path length never got a vote.

Step 3: pick the right lever. You have three tools for inbound traffic, in increasing strength:

  1. AS_PATH prepending. Announce via Ashburn as 64496 64496 64496. This only wins at step 4. It does nothing against a network whose LOCAL_PREF already prefers the Ashburn path, which is the exact network you need to move. Prepending also has diminishing returns; beyond three or so, it mostly just makes your routes look strange.
  2. Provider action communities. Your Ashburn transit, 64498, publishes communities that change its behaviour. Its documentation says 64498:2120 means "do not announce to customers in the EU region" (the exact values differ for every provider; read their documentation). Tag your Ashburn announcement with it, and 64506 stops learning your route through 64498 at all.
  3. More-specifics. Announce 203.0.112.0/24 and 203.0.113.0/24 only in Frankfurt, keeping the /22 everywhere. Longest-prefix match then sends all traffic for those /24s to Frankfurt from anywhere that accepts them. This is the strongest tool and the bluntest. It adds routes to everyone's table. And it depends on your ROA: with maxLength /22 instead of /24, every network doing ROV would see your /24s as Invalid and drop them. The prefix would vanish exactly where you meant to steer it.

You choose option 2, because it is targeted, and you prepend once on the Ashburn session as a second nudge.

Step 4: verify. Re-query the same looking glass ten minutes later:

lg> show ip bgp 203.0.112.0/22
Paths: (1 available, best #1)
  64505 64497 64496
    Origin IGP, localpref 100, valid, external, best

The Ashburn path is gone from this network's view. Over the next hour your Frankfurt port climbs to 5.8 Gbit/s and Ashburn falls to 4.3. European latency drops back.

What the example teaches. Outbound traffic is yours to steer: set LOCAL_PREF and you're done. Inbound traffic is the product of other people's step 2. You can only hint at their decisions (prepending, MED), ask your direct neighbours to change theirs (communities), or overrule them with longest-prefix match (more-specifics). And every one of those hints is checked against your ROA.

6. Misconceptions & common mistakes

"BGP picks the shortest path." It picks the most preferred path. LOCAL_PREF runs before AS_PATH length, and AS_PATH length counts networks, not kilometres or milliseconds. A three-AS path through a customer beats a one-AS path through a provider every time.

"Prepending will move my inbound traffic." Only for networks where everything up to step 4 ties. Any network that sets LOCAL_PREF by relationship (which is all of them) ignores your prepends whenever the two paths come from different relationship classes. Prepending is a hint. Communities and more-specifics are instructions.

"BGP is a routing protocol, so it finds failures quickly." BGP finds out about failures when TCP or the hold timer tells it. With a 90-second hold time and no BFD, a silently dead neighbour keeps attracting traffic for up to 90 seconds. Then withdrawal path-hunting adds more time on top.

"RPKI stops hijacks." ROV stops one class of hijack: a wrong origin AS, or a more-specific beyond the ROA's maxLength. It does nothing for leaks, and nothing for an attacker who forges the path with your ASN at the end. It also does nothing if your upstream doesn't drop Invalids.

"A /24 announced anywhere is reachable everywhere." It is reachable through networks that accept it. Prefixes longer than /24 (IPv4) or /48 (IPv6) are filtered almost everywhere. Prefixes with no matching IRR object, or that are RPKI-Invalid, are filtered by a growing number of networks. "I announced it" and "the internet accepted it" are different claims; a looking glass tells you which one is true.

"Anycast sends users to the nearest server." It sends them to the server at the end of the path their ISP preferred. That is usually close, sometimes absurdly far, and it can change when anyone between the user and you changes policy.

7. Interview Q&A

Q1: What is the difference between eBGP and iBGP, and why does iBGP need a full mesh?

eBGP runs between different ASes: it prepends the local ASN and rewrites NEXT_HOP. iBGP runs inside one AS: it leaves AS_PATH and NEXT_HOP alone. Because AS_PATH doesn't change inside the AS, iBGP can't use it to detect loops. So it uses a rule instead: never pass an iBGP-learned route to another iBGP peer. That rule means each router must hear every external route directly from the router that learned it, which means a full mesh. Route reflectors relax the rule for designated clients and add ORIGINATOR_ID and CLUSTER_LIST to keep loop detection working.

Q2: Two routes to the same prefix: one has LOCAL_PREF 200 and AS_PATH length 5, the other LOCAL_PREF 100 and length 1. Which wins?

The LOCAL_PREF 200 route. LOCAL_PREF is compared at step 2; AS_PATH length isn't looked at until step 4. Add that LOCAL_PREF is your own AS's policy, typically set from the business relationship, so this is exactly what you want: a long path through a paying customer beats a short path through a provider you pay.

Q3: What's the difference between a route leak and a hijack?

A hijack is an announcement by an AS that isn't authorised to originate the prefix: the origin is wrong. A leak is a real route, with the right origin, sent where the export policy says it shouldn't go, for example a provider's routes passed to another provider. ROV can catch a hijack with the wrong origin. It cannot catch a leak. Leak defences are prefix filters and max-prefix limits on customer sessions, RFC 9234 roles with the OTC attribute, and in future ASPA.

Q4: You announce 203.0.113.0/24 and someone else announces 203.0.113.0/25 and 203.0.113.128/25. What happens?

Wherever the /25s are accepted, they win by longest-prefix match, regardless of any BGP attribute; BGP never even compares them with your /24 because they are different prefixes. In practice most networks filter IPv4 prefixes longer than /24, so the /25s propagate less widely. And if you have a ROA for the /24 with maxLength 24, the /25s are RPKI-Invalid and networks doing ROV drop them. That's two independent reasons a /25 hijack fails today. Neither the /24 filter nor path length helps against a /24 hijack of a /22 you announce; only a ROA with a tight maxLength makes that one Invalid.

Q5: A BGP session flaps between Active and Connect and never reaches Established. What do you check?

Those states mean the TCP connection is not completing. Check reachability to the peer address from the right source address, a firewall or ACL dropping TCP 179, the eBGP TTL (a neighbour that is not directly connected needs multihop), and an MD5/TCP-AO password mismatch, which makes the receiving kernel silently drop segments. tcpdump -nn port 179 on the router tells you within seconds whether SYNs leave and whether anything comes back.

9. Code: a toy best-path selector

This script implements the RFC 4271 decision process (§3.8) as a sequence of eliminations, so you can watch which step decides each contest. It leaves out the vendor-specific steps (Cisco weight, oldest eBGP path) and multipath. Python 3.13, stdlib only. bestpath.py:

"""A toy BGP best-path selector (RFC 4271 9.1.2.2 + RFC 4456 tie-breaks).

Not a router: no Cisco "weight", no "oldest eBGP path" step, no multipath.
Each step removes candidates; the explanation shows who fell out and why.
"""
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from ipaddress import ip_address

ORIGIN_RANK = {"i": 0, "e": 1, "?": 2}          # IGP < EGP < INCOMPLETE


@dataclass
class Route:
    name: str
    peer_ip: str
    router_id: str
    session: str                 # "ebgp", "ibgp" or "local"
    as_path: list = field(default_factory=list)
    local_pref: int = 100        # default LOCAL_PREF on most implementations
    origin: str = "i"
    med: int | None = None       # missing MED is treated as 0 (RFC 4271)
    igp_cost: int = 0            # IGP metric to the BGP NEXT_HOP
    nh_reachable: bool = True
    cluster_len: int = 0         # length of CLUSTER_LIST (reflected routes)

    @property
    def neighbor_as(self):
        return self.as_path[0] if self.as_path else None

    def short(self):
        path = " ".join(map(str, self.as_path)) or "(local)"
        med = "-" if self.med is None else self.med
        return (f"{self.name:<9} lp={self.local_pref:<3} path=[{path}] "
                f"origin={self.origin} med={med} {self.session} igp={self.igp_cost}")


def keep_min(cands, key):
    best = min(key(r) for r in cands)
    return [r for r in cands if key(r) == best]


def select(routes, verbose=True):
    """Elimination in the RFC order. Returns the winner."""
    steps = [
        ("next hop reachable", lambda c: [r for r in c if r.nh_reachable]),
        ("highest LOCAL_PREF", lambda c: keep_min(c, lambda r: -r.local_pref)),
        ("locally originated", lambda c: keep_min(c, lambda r: r.session != "local")),
        ("shortest AS_PATH", lambda c: keep_min(c, lambda r: len(r.as_path))),
        ("lowest ORIGIN", lambda c: keep_min(c, lambda r: ORIGIN_RANK[r.origin])),
        ("lowest MED (per neighbour AS)", med_step),
        ("eBGP over iBGP", lambda c: keep_min(c, lambda r: r.session != "ebgp")),
        ("lowest IGP cost to next hop", lambda c: keep_min(c, lambda r: r.igp_cost)),
        ("lowest router ID", lambda c: keep_min(c, lambda r: ip_address(r.router_id))),
        ("shortest CLUSTER_LIST", lambda c: keep_min(c, lambda r: r.cluster_len)),
        ("lowest peer address", lambda c: keep_min(c, lambda r: ip_address(r.peer_ip))),
    ]
    cands = list(routes)
    for n, (label, fn) in enumerate(steps, 1):
        after = fn(cands)
        gone = [r.name for r in cands if r not in after]
        if verbose and gone:
            print(f"  step {n:>2} {label:<30} drops {', '.join(gone)}")
        cands = after
        if len(cands) == 1:
            if verbose:
                print(f"  => best: {cands[0].name} (decided at step {n}: {label})")
            return cands[0]
    raise AssertionError("tie survived every step")


def med_step(cands):
    """MED only compares routes learned from the same neighbouring AS."""
    survivors = []
    for nas in dict.fromkeys(r.neighbor_as for r in cands):
        group = [r for r in cands if r.neighbor_as == nas]
        survivors += keep_min(group, lambda r: r.med or 0)
    return [r for r in cands if r in survivors]


def pairwise_better(a, b):
    """One-on-one comparison, the way a router walks paths in arrival order
    when 'deterministic MED' is off. MED is consulted only if same neighbour AS."""
    if a.local_pref != b.local_pref:
        return a if a.local_pref > b.local_pref else b
    if len(a.as_path) != len(b.as_path):
        return a if len(a.as_path) < len(b.as_path) else b
    if a.neighbor_as == b.neighbor_as and (a.med or 0) != (b.med or 0):
        return a if (a.med or 0) < (b.med or 0) else b
    if a.igp_cost != b.igp_cost:
        return a if a.igp_cost < b.igp_cost else b
    return a if ip_address(a.router_id) < ip_address(b.router_id) else b


def show(title, routes):
    print(f"\n{title}")
    for r in routes:
        print("  " + r.short())
    return select(routes)


# Scenario 1: policy beats path length --------------------------------------
# AS 64496 buys transit from 64497, peers with 64498, sells transit to 64499.
# Inbound policy stamps LOCAL_PREF: customer 200, peer 150, transit 100.
s1 = [
    Route("transit", "192.0.2.1", "10.0.0.1", "ebgp", [64497, 64511], 100),
    Route("peer", "192.0.2.5", "10.0.0.7", "ebgp", [64498, 64511], 150),
    Route("customer", "192.0.2.9", "10.0.0.9", "ebgp",
          [64499, 64499, 64499, 64511], 200),
]
show("Scenario 1: 203.0.113.0/24 seen from transit, peer and customer", s1)

# Scenario 2: two equal transit routes, learned at two different border
# routers; this router hears one over eBGP and one over iBGP.
s2 = [
    Route("via-edge1", "192.0.2.1", "10.0.0.1", "ebgp", [64497, 64511],
          med=50, igp_cost=0),
    Route("via-edge2", "10.255.0.2", "10.255.0.2", "ibgp", [64501, 64511],
          med=0, igp_cost=20),
]
show("Scenario 2: equal policy, equal length, different upstream ASes", s2)

# Scenario 3: same upstream AS at two exits -> MED is comparable,
# both learned over iBGP, so MED decides before IGP cost.
s3 = [
    Route("exit-fra", "10.255.0.11", "10.255.0.11", "ibgp", [64497, 64511],
          med=200, igp_cost=5),
    Route("exit-ams", "10.255.0.12", "10.255.0.12", "ibgp", [64497, 64511],
          med=10, igp_cost=40),
]
show("Scenario 3: one upstream AS, two exits, MED says 'use Amsterdam'", s3)

# Scenario 4: the MED non-transitivity trap -------------------------------
A = Route("A", "10.255.0.1", "10.255.0.1", "ibgp", [64497, 64511], med=200, igp_cost=5)
B = Route("B", "10.255.0.2", "10.255.0.2", "ibgp", [64498, 64511], med=50, igp_cost=10)
C = Route("C", "10.255.0.3", "10.255.0.3", "ibgp", [64497, 64511], med=100, igp_cost=15)
print("\nScenario 4: three iBGP paths, MED comparable only between A and C")
for r in (A, B, C):
    print("  " + r.short())
for x, y in ((A, B), (B, C), (A, C)):
    print(f"  pairwise {x.name} vs {y.name}: {pairwise_better(x, y).name}")
for order in ((A, B, C), (B, C, A), (C, A, B)):
    best = order[0]
    for r in order[1:]:
        best = pairwise_better(best, r)
    print(f"  arrival order {''.join(r.name for r in order)} -> pairwise walk picks {best.name}")
print("  grouped by neighbour AS (deterministic MED):")
select([A, B, C])

Read the four scenarios in order.

Scenario 1 is the Gao–Rexford rule in action. The customer's route has been prepended twice and is the longest path on offer. It still wins, at step 2, because import policy gave it LOCAL_PREF 200. Path length never got a look.

Scenario 2 ties all the way to step 6. MED can't separate the two routes because they come from different neighbouring ASes (64497 and 64501), and comparing their MEDs would be meaningless. So the decision falls to step 7, where the route learned directly over eBGP beats the one carried over iBGP from another border router. That is "get traffic off my network at the nearest exit," expressed as a rule.

Scenario 3 shows the one place MED does work: two links to the same neighbour AS. The upstream says, through MED, "please use Amsterdam," and step 6 obeys before IGP cost (which would have picked Frankfurt) is consulted.

Scenario 4 is the subtle one, and a favourite senior interview question. Because MED only applies within one neighbour AS, "better than" stops being transitive. A beats B on IGP cost, B beats C on IGP cost, and C beats A on MED. A router that compares paths pairwise in the order they arrived picks a different winner depending on that order. Three orders give three different answers. Two routers in the same AS can then disagree on the best path, and in route-reflector topologies that can cause persistent route oscillation (RFC 3345 documents this). The fix is what the elimination function does: group paths by neighbouring AS, apply MED within each group, then compare the survivors. Cisco calls that bgp deterministic-med; turn it on, or use always-compare-med if all your neighbours set MED consistently.

Deep dive: what the summary view leaves out

The first nine sections give you the protocol as operators describe it. The deep dive goes underneath. It covers the data structures inside a BGP speaker, the actual bytes on a real session, the numbers that size the system, and the ways it breaks in production, using real incidents rather than hypotheticals. Then come the places BGP turns up in a 2026 stack, and the interview questions that separate people who have run BGP from people who have read about it.

Deep dive: the real mental model

Picture a BGP speaker not as "a router that runs BGP" but as a pipeline of tables with policy in between. RFC 4271 §3.2 names three Routing Information Bases, and every implementation keeps something close to them.

Adj-RIB-In, one per neighbour, holds what that neighbour sent, before your policy touches it. Many implementations only keep the post-policy version to save memory. That's why "soft reconfiguration inbound" exists as an option: it keeps a pre-policy copy so you can change import policy without asking the neighbour to resend. Route refresh (RFC 2918) made that copy largely unnecessary, because you can simply ask the neighbour to send everything again.

Import policy is where your network's intent lives. Prefix lists drop bogons and anything too specific. AS-path filters drop paths containing networks you never want to transit. ROV drops Invalids. Max-prefix limits tear the session down if a neighbour suddenly sends far more routes than expected. And route maps set LOCAL_PREF and communities from the relationship. RFC 8212 says an eBGP session with no import or export policy configured should accept and send nothing. FRR enforces this by default (bgp ebgp-requires-policy), which is why a new FRR eBGP session comes up Established and exchanges zero prefixes until you add a policy.

Loc-RIB holds every path that survived import, grouped by prefix, with one marked best. Every time a path for a prefix is added, changed or removed, the decision process reruns for that prefix only. This per-prefix scope is why BGP handles a million prefixes: a change to one prefix costs one small comparison, not a global recomputation. (Contrast OSPF, where one link change triggers an SPF run over the whole area.)

RIB and FIB. The best BGP path competes with routes from other sources (connected, static, OSPF) for a place in the router's main routing table. Vendors break that tie with administrative distance (Cisco: eBGP 20, OSPF 110, iBGP 200). Linux's kernel has no such concept, so FRR's zebra daemon does the arbitration before it programs the kernel FIB through netlink. On hardware routers the FIB is pushed down into ASIC memory (TCAM or algorithmic lookup tables). And that memory is finite, which matters below.

Export policy and Adj-RIB-Out. For each neighbour, the router runs export policy over the Loc-RIB's best paths and records what it decided to send. Only best paths are exported by default. If your best path for a prefix came from a peer, you export nothing to your other peers and transits, even if you also hold a customer path you could legally export. (Add-path, RFC 7911, lets a speaker send more than one path per prefix, mostly used between route reflectors and their clients.)

Three practical consequences follow from this picture:

  1. "I see the route in show bgp but traffic doesn't use it." It's in Loc-RIB but either lost best-path, lost to a lower-distance route in the RIB, or failed to program into the FIB. Check each table in turn: show bgp <prefix>, then show ip route <prefix>, then the hardware or kernel FIB (ip route get <addr> on Linux).
  2. Next-hop tracking ties BGP to the IGP. Every BGP path depends on its NEXT_HOP resolving through the RIB. When an IGP change makes a next hop unreachable, every BGP path using it becomes ineligible at once, even though no BGP message has arrived. Modern implementations react to that within milliseconds of the IGP change. Prefix-independent convergence (BGP PIC) goes further: the FIB keeps a pre-computed backup next hop shared by all affected prefixes, so failover is one pointer change rather than a million route rewrites.
  3. Every change is per-prefix, but updates are packed per attribute set. One UPDATE carries one set of path attributes and every prefix that shares it. A full table of a million prefixes typically packs into a few hundred thousand UPDATE messages, because so many prefixes share identical attributes.

127.0.0.1.179 > 127.0.0.2.46887: Flags [P.], seq 65:86, ack 169, length 21: BGP
    Notification Message (3), length: 21, Cease (6), subcode Administrative Shutdown (2)
127.0.0.1.179 > 127.0.0.2.46887: Flags [F.], seq 86, ack 169, ...   <- NOTIFICATION, then close

The numbers all add up. Each OPEN is 45 bytes (19 header + 10 fixed fields + 16 bytes of optional parameters). The two OPENs plus two KEEPALIVEs advance each side's sequence numbers by exactly 45 + 19 = 64, so both sides' next data starts at relative sequence 65. The 104-byte segment is 54 + 23 + 27. The NOTIFICATION is the 19-byte header plus a code byte and a subcode byte.

Four things in this capture are worth carrying around.

BGP messages are not packets. TCP is a byte stream, and the three UPDATEs travelled in one segment. A big UPDATE can equally span several segments. The receiver finds message boundaries from the 2-byte length field after the marker. This is why a BGP decoder that assumes "one message per packet" gives garbage on a busy session, and why a TCP problem (a black-holed MSS, a retransmission storm) stalls BGP as a whole.

The hold time is the minimum of the two offers. Neither side "wins"; both use 30 s here and send KEEPALIVEs every 10 s if idle. An offer of 0 means "no keepalives at all", and a value of 1 or 2 is illegal and rejected with OPEN error subcode 6.

A withdrawal carries only the prefix. No attributes are needed to say "forget this." The withdraw UPDATE here is 27 bytes: 19 header, 2 bytes of withdrawn length, the 4-byte prefix, 2 bytes of zero attribute length. tcpdump also prints "End-of-Rib Marker" under it. That is a decoder quirk: it labels any UPDATE with no NLRI that way. A true End-of-RIB (RFC 4724) has no withdrawals and no attributes either, like the 23-byte message before it.

Closing is explicit. A NOTIFICATION precedes the FIN, so the peer logs a reason. If you see a session drop with no NOTIFICATION in a capture, the TCP connection died underneath BGP: a reset from a middlebox, a hold-timer expiry on one side only, or a dead link.

Here is the script that produced the capture. It speaks just enough BGP to bring a session up and exchange routes. It's no router, but every byte it sends is valid RFC 4271, which is why tcpdump decodes it.

"""Two toy BGP speakers on loopback: OPEN, KEEPALIVE, UPDATE, End-of-RIB,
withdraw, NOTIFICATION. Real RFC 4271 bytes, so tcpdump decodes them.

Usage: python3 bgp_wire.py [port]      (port 179 needs root; default 1179)
On Linux 127.0.0.2 is already a loopback address. On macOS run
`sudo ifconfig lo0 alias 127.0.0.2` first (remove with `-alias`).
"""
import socket
import struct
import sys
import threading
from ipaddress import ip_address, ip_network

PORT = int(sys.argv[1]) if len(sys.argv) > 1 else 1179
MARKER = b"\xff" * 16
TYPES = {1: "OPEN", 2: "UPDATE", 3: "NOTIFICATION", 4: "KEEPALIVE"}


def msg(mtype, body=b""):
    return MARKER + struct.pack("!HB", 19 + len(body), mtype) + body


def open_msg(asn, hold, router_id):
    caps = (b"\x01\x04" + struct.pack("!HBB", 1, 0, 1)    # MP-BGP: IPv4 unicast
            + b"\x02\x00"                                  # route refresh
            + b"\x41\x04" + struct.pack("!I", asn))        # 4-octet AS number
    opt = b"\x02" + bytes([len(caps)]) + caps               # one Capabilities param
    my_as = asn if asn < 65536 else 23456                   # AS_TRANS if needed
    body = struct.pack("!BHH", 4, my_as, hold) + ip_address(router_id).packed
    return msg(1, body + bytes([len(opt)]) + opt)


def attr(flags, code, value):
    return struct.pack("!BBB", flags, code, len(value)) + value


def nlri(prefix):
    net = ip_network(prefix)
    nbytes = (net.prefixlen + 7) // 8
    return bytes([net.prefixlen]) + net.network_address.packed[:nbytes]


def update(withdrawn=(), announce=(), as_path=(), next_hop=None, communities=()):
    wd = b"".join(nlri(p) for p in withdrawn)
    attrs = b""
    if announce:
        seg = struct.pack("!BB", 2, len(as_path)) + b"".join(
            struct.pack("!I", a) for a in as_path)          # AS_SEQUENCE, 4-byte ASNs
        attrs += attr(0x40, 1, b"\x00")                      # ORIGIN = IGP
        attrs += attr(0x40, 2, seg)                          # AS_PATH
        attrs += attr(0x40, 3, ip_address(next_hop).packed)  # NEXT_HOP
        if communities:
            attrs += attr(0xC0, 8, b"".join(
                struct.pack("!HH", *c) for c in communities))
    body = (struct.pack("!H", len(wd)) + wd + struct.pack("!H", len(attrs))
            + attrs + b"".join(nlri(p) for p in announce))
    return msg(2, body)


def read_msg(sock):
    hdr = b""
    while len(hdr) < 19:
        chunk = sock.recv(19 - len(hdr))
        if not chunk:
            return None, None
        hdr += chunk
    length, mtype = struct.unpack("!HB", hdr[16:19])
    body = b""
    while len(body) < length - 19:
        body += sock.recv(length - 19 - len(body))
    return mtype, body


def describe(mtype, body):
    if mtype == 1:
        ver, my_as, hold = struct.unpack("!BHH", body[:5])
        rid = ip_address(body[5:9])
        return f"OPEN v{ver} AS{my_as} hold={hold}s id={rid} optlen={body[9]}"
    if mtype == 2:
        wlen = struct.unpack("!H", body[:2])[0]
        alen = struct.unpack("!H", body[2 + wlen:4 + wlen])[0]
        if wlen == 0 and alen == 0:
            return "UPDATE (empty: End-of-RIB for IPv4 unicast)"
        return f"UPDATE withdrawn_len={wlen} attrs_len={alen} nlri_len={len(body) - 4 - wlen - alen}"
    if mtype == 3:
        return f"NOTIFICATION code={body[0]} subcode={body[1]}"
    return TYPES.get(mtype, f"type {mtype}")


log_lock = threading.Lock()


def log(who, text):
    with log_lock:
        print(f"{who}: {text}", flush=True)


def r1_server(ready):
    """AS 64496, router ID 192.0.2.1: the 'passive' side."""
    srv = socket.socket()
    srv.setsockopt(socket.SOL_SOCKET, socket.SO_REUSEADDR, 1)
    srv.bind(("127.0.0.1", PORT))
    srv.listen(1)
    log("R1", f"Idle -> Active (listening on 127.0.0.1:{PORT})")
    ready.set()
    conn, peer = srv.accept()
    log("R1", f"TCP up from {peer[0]}:{peer[1]}")
    mtype, body = read_msg(conn)
    log("R1", "rx " + describe(mtype, body))
    conn.sendall(open_msg(64496, 90, "192.0.2.1"))
    peer_hold = struct.unpack("!H", body[3:5])[0]
    hold = min(90, peer_hold)
    log("R1", f"tx OPEN; Active -> OpenConfirm; negotiated hold={hold}s keepalive={hold // 3}s")
    conn.sendall(msg(4))
    rib = {}
    while True:
        mtype, body = read_msg(conn)
        if mtype is None:
            break
        log("R1", "rx " + describe(mtype, body))
        if mtype == 4 and not rib.get("_up"):
            rib["_up"] = True
            log("R1", "OpenConfirm -> Established")
        if mtype == 2:
            wlen = struct.unpack("!H", body[:2])[0]
            alen = struct.unpack("!H", body[2 + wlen:4 + wlen])[0]
            if wlen:
                log("R1", "  Adj-RIB-In: withdraw 203.0.113.0/24")
                rib.pop("203.0.113.0/24", None)
            elif alen:
                rib["203.0.113.0/24"] = "AS_PATH 64511"
                log("R1", "  Adj-RIB-In: add 203.0.113.0/24 AS_PATH 64511")
            if wlen:
                conn.sendall(msg(3, b"\x06\x02"))    # Cease / Administrative Shutdown
                log("R1", "tx NOTIFICATION Cease/Admin Shutdown; Established -> Idle")
                break
    conn.close()
    srv.close()


def r2_client():
    """AS 64511, router ID 192.0.2.2: originates 203.0.113.0/24."""
    s = socket.socket()
    s.bind(("127.0.0.2", 0))
    s.connect(("127.0.0.1", PORT))
    log("R2", "Connect -> OpenSent (tx OPEN hold=30)")
    s.sendall(open_msg(64511, 30, "192.0.2.2"))
    mtype, body = read_msg(s)
    log("R2", "rx " + describe(mtype, body))
    s.sendall(msg(4))
    mtype, body = read_msg(s)
    log("R2", "rx " + describe(mtype, body) + "; OpenConfirm -> Established")
    s.sendall(update(announce=["203.0.113.0/24"], as_path=[64511],
                     next_hop="127.0.0.2", communities=[(64511, 100)]))
    s.sendall(update())                                   # End-of-RIB marker
    s.sendall(update(withdrawn=["203.0.113.0/24"]))
    mtype, body = read_msg(s)
    log("R2", "rx " + describe(mtype, body))
    s.close()


if __name__ == "__main__":
    ready = threading.Event()
    t = threading.Thread(target=r1_server, args=(ready,))
    t.start()
    ready.wait()
    r2_client()
    t.join()
    sizes = {"OPEN": len(open_msg(64496, 90, "192.0.2.1")), "KEEPALIVE": len(msg(4)),
             "UPDATE(announce)": len(update(announce=["203.0.113.0/24"], as_path=[64511],
                                            next_hop="127.0.0.2", communities=[(64511, 100)])),
             "UPDATE(End-of-RIB)": len(update()),
             "UPDATE(withdraw)": len(update(withdrawn=["203.0.113.0/24"])),
             "NOTIFICATION": len(msg(3, b"\x06\x02"))}
    print("message sizes (bytes):", ", ".join(f"{k}={v}" for k, v in sizes.items()))

Worked example: how big is a full-table transfer? Assume 1 million IPv4 prefixes, and that, after packing, an average UPDATE carries four prefixes and 60 bytes of attributes. A /24 encodes in 4 bytes. That's 250,000 UPDATEs × (19 + 4 + 60 + 4 × 4 = 99 bytes) ≈ 25 MB. Over a fast link that's under a second of transfer. In practice an initial full-table load takes from a few seconds to a few minutes, because the receiver's CPU, not the link, is the bottleneck: every prefix needs policy evaluation, best-path, and a FIB write. That's also why a router reloading with eight full-table peers is noticeably slower to converge than one with two.

Worked example: the cost of a hold timer. A link carrying 4 Gbit/s fails in a way the interface doesn't notice (a dead optical amplifier, a broken provider switch in the middle). With a 90 s hold timer, BGP keeps pointing traffic at the dead next hop for up to 90 s. That's 4 × 10⁹ × 90 / 8 = 45 GB of traffic dropped. With BFD at 300 ms × 3, the same failure costs about 450 MB. That ratio is why BFD is standard on every serious eBGP link.

The FIB has a ceiling. On 12 August 2014 the global IPv4 table briefly passed 512,000 routes. A lot of widely deployed routers (Cisco Catalyst 6500 and 7600 series with certain supervisor cards, for example) had their TCAM partitioned for 512k IPv4 routes by default. Past that, they fell back to software forwarding or dropped routes, and outages followed at a number of ISPs. The fix was a config change and a reload to re-partition the TCAM. The general lesson still applies: the table keeps growing, and hardware FIB capacity is a number to check before it checks you.

Deep dive: production failure modes

Each case below follows the same shape: symptom, mechanism, how to prove it, fix. The internet incidents are described as their operators and independent analysts reported them. Where details are disputed, the text says so.

Route leak: Verizon, DQE and a BGP optimiser (24 June 2019)

Symptom. For about two hours, large parts of Cloudflare, AWS, Linode and many other networks were slow or unreachable for some users.

Mechanism. DQE Communications (AS33154) ran a "BGP optimiser", software that generates more-specific routes for internal traffic engineering. Those more-specifics leaked to DQE's customer Allegheny Technologies (AS396531). Allegheny was also a Verizon customer, and it re-announced them to Verizon (AS701). Verizon accepted them from a customer with no prefix filter and no effective max-prefix limit, and passed them to the rest of the internet. Because they were more-specifics, they won by longest-prefix match wherever they were accepted. Huge volumes of traffic headed for a small steel company's links and died there. This is the classic valley: provider routes from one upstream leaked to another upstream.

How to prove it. From outside, a looking glass or bgp.tools shows unfamiliar more-specifics of your prefixes, with a path like ... 701 396531 33154 <your ASN>. A route-monitoring tool (BGPalerter, bgp.tools alerts, Cloudflare Radar's route-leak detection) fires on "new more-specific" and "unexpected upstream". From inside, show bgp <your-prefix> longer-prefixes on a router that accepts the full table shows the intruders.

Fix. For you as a victim: call the networks involved, and in parallel announce your own matching more-specifics to compete (as long as your ROA allows them). For the network that should have stopped it: prefix filters on every customer session, built from IRR data with a tool like bgpq4, plus a max-prefix limit. RFC 9234 roles would have caught it too, because Allegheny would have been marked as a customer that must not send provider routes upward.

Hijack by more-specific: Pakistan Telecom and YouTube (24 February 2008)

Symptom. YouTube was unreachable from most of the internet for roughly two hours.

Mechanism. The Pakistani government ordered YouTube blocked inside the country. Pakistan Telecom (AS17557) chose to do it by announcing 208.65.153.0/24, a more-specific of YouTube's 208.65.152.0/22, intending to blackhole it domestically. The announcement leaked to its upstream PCCW (AS3491), which propagated it worldwide. Every network that accepted the /24 sent YouTube's traffic to Pakistan. YouTube responded by announcing the same /24 itself, and then two /25s, to win back traffic by longest-prefix match. The hijack ended when PCCW withdrew the route.

What would stop it today. A ROA for 208.65.152.0/22 with maxLength 22, plus ROV at PCCW or its peers, would have made the /24 Invalid on arrival. Even without RPKI, an IRR-based customer prefix filter at PCCW would have stopped it.

Hijack for theft: Amazon Route 53 and MyEtherWallet (24 April 2018)

Symptom. For about two hours, some users of the cryptocurrency wallet site myetherwallet.com reached a phishing copy of it.

Mechanism. Attackers got more-specific /24 routes for parts of Amazon Route 53's authoritative DNS address space announced through eNet (AS10297), an ISP in Ohio. Resolvers whose upstreams accepted those routes sent DNS queries for myetherwallet.com to the attackers' server instead of to Route 53, and got back the address of a server in Russia. That server presented a self-signed TLS certificate. Users who clicked through the browser warning had their wallets drained. Google Public DNS was among the resolvers that received the bad route for a time.

Lessons. BGP hijacks are a way to attack DNS, and DNS is how you attack everything else. TLS held: every victim had to click past a certificate warning. And Amazon has since published ROAs for this space, so the same /24s from the wrong origin would now be Invalid wherever ROV is enforced.

Mass hijack: China Telecom (8 April 2010)

For about 18 minutes, China Telecom (AS23724) announced around 37,000 prefixes belonging to other networks, roughly 15% of the routing table at the time, including some US government and military prefixes. Only some networks accepted the routes, and how much traffic was actually diverted is disputed. The widely accepted explanation is a misconfiguration. It is still the standard example of how far one mistake can reach. An older one is AS7007 on 25 April 1997, when a router at MAI Network Services de-aggregated much of the routing table into /24s, re-announced them as its own, and pulled large parts of the internet's traffic into a small network for hours.

Self-inflicted withdrawal: Facebook (4 October 2021)

Symptom. facebook.com, Instagram and WhatsApp unreachable for about six hours. DNS lookups for them failed worldwide.

Mechanism. A command issued during routine maintenance, meant to assess global backbone capacity, took down all of Facebook's backbone connections. An audit tool should have stopped the command but had a bug. Facebook's authoritative DNS servers are designed to withdraw their anycast BGP announcements whenever they can't reach the data centres, on the theory that an unhealthy site should stop attracting queries. Every site lost the backbone at once, so every site withdrew. The prefixes holding Facebook's nameservers disappeared from the global table. Recovery was slow because the tools engineers would normally use for remote repair depended on the same network, and staff had to get physical access to data centre equipment.

How it looked from outside. Route collectors showed a burst of withdrawals from AS32934 around 15:40 UTC. dig @a.ns.facebook.com facebook.com timed out, because there was no route to the nameserver's address at all. That is a very different failure from SERVFAIL caused by a broken zone.

Lesson. Health-check-driven withdrawal is a powerful anycast pattern with a nasty correlated-failure mode: if every site reaches the same conclusion at once, you withdraw yourself from the internet. Protect against it with a minimum-sites floor ("never withdraw the last N"), or by keeping at least one announcement statically pinned.

Your own ROA eats your announcement

Symptom. You announce a new more-specific for traffic engineering, as in §5. Traffic for it barely moves. Some networks see it, many don't.

Mechanism. Your ROA covers the aggregate with maxLength equal to the aggregate length, so the more-specific is RPKI-Invalid. Every network enforcing ROV drops it.

How to prove it. Use the RIPEstat RPKI validation query from §8 for the exact prefix and origin; it returns invalid with reason as or length. Validators show it locally too: routinator vrps lists the validated ROA payloads, and Routinator's validate subcommand checks a single route and origin (see routinator validate --help).

Fix. Publish a ROA that covers exactly the more-specifics you announce. RFC 9319 advises against a loose maxLength (like /24 on a /16). A loose maxLength lets a hijacker announce any more-specific with your origin ASN forged at the end of the path and still look Valid.

The session that comes up and then dies (MTU)

Symptom. A new eBGP session reaches Established, starts receiving the full table, then hangs. After exactly one hold time it drops with "hold timer expired." Then it repeats, forever.

Mechanism. OPEN and KEEPALIVE are small. Full-table UPDATEs fill TCP segments to the MSS. Somewhere on the path the real MTU is lower than the endpoints think: a GRE or IPsec tunnel, a misconfigured MTU on one side of a link. Large segments are dropped, and the ICMP "fragmentation needed" that should trigger Path MTU Discovery is filtered or never generated. TCP retransmits the same large segment forever. Small KEEPALIVEs can't get past it in the stream, so the hold timer expires.

How to prove it. tcpdump -nn -i <if> 'tcp port 179' shows repeated retransmissions of one full-sized segment. ping -D -s 1472 <peer> on macOS (ping -M do -s 1472 <peer> on Linux) fails while smaller sizes succeed.

Fix. Make the MTU consistent end to end, or lower the TCP MSS for BGP (most implementations have a per-neighbour tcp-mss setting, or enable PMTUD for BGP).

Deep dive: 2026 context

BGP as the data-centre routing protocol

Large data centres stopped using OSPF in the fabric years ago. RFC 7938 describes the design most of them use: a Clos (leaf-spine) topology where every link runs eBGP, and BGP is the only routing protocol.

Why eBGP, when the whole thing is one organisation? Because eBGP's behaviour fits a fabric. Each tier gets private ASNs (often 4-byte ones, from the 4200000000+ range, so there are plenty). Spines in one fabric commonly share an ASN, so AS_PATH loop detection automatically rejects paths that would bounce leaf → spine → leaf → spine. Every link is a simple point-to-point session, with no IGP flooding domain whose failure takes out the whole data centre. Multipath is natural. A leaf learns a server prefix from every spine with equal AS_PATH length and installs all of them for ECMP; bestpath as-path multipath-relax lets it do so even when the paths cross different ASNs.

Two refinements show up in most modern fabrics. BGP unnumbered (an FRR and Cumulus innovation) runs sessions over IPv6 link-local addresses discovered by router advertisements, so nobody assigns a /31 to every link. IPv4 prefixes are carried with IPv6 next hops using RFC 8950 (which obsoleted RFC 5549). And EVPN (RFC 7432, with RFC 8365 for VXLAN) uses BGP as the control plane for overlay networks, carrying MAC and IP reachability so tenant Layer 2 segments can stretch across a Layer 3 fabric without flooding.

Kubernetes: Calico, MetalLB, Cilium

Inside a bare-metal or on-prem Kubernetes cluster, BGP is how pod and service addresses become reachable from the physical network without tunnels.

Calico runs a BGP daemon (BIRD) inside each calico-node pod. Calico's IPAM gives each node one or more address blocks (/26 by default) for its pods, and the node announces those blocks. Out of the box the nodes form a full iBGP mesh using AS 64512; that suits clusters up to roughly 100 nodes. Larger clusters switch the mesh off and use route reflectors, or peer each node with its top-of-rack leaf over eBGP, which is the design in the diagram. It needs an IPAddressPool and a BGPAdvertisement alongside it before anything is announced.

The production gotcha is in the router, not in MetalLB. When the set of nodes announcing a /32 changes (a node drains, a pod moves), the router's ECMP group changes size. Most routers rehash many existing flows onto different next hops, and the new node has no conntrack state for them, so long-lived TCP connections reset. Resilient (consistent) hashing on the router limits the damage, where the hardware supports it. Otherwise expect connection resets on every rollout. MetalLB's documentation discusses this limitation directly.

Cilium has its own BGP control plane (built on GoBGP) that does the same jobs, announcing pod CIDRs and LoadBalancer IPs, configured through Cilium's CRDs.

Clouds and BGP

You rarely run BGP inside a cloud VPC; the provider's SDN does the routing. You meet it at the edges: AWS Direct Connect and Site-to-Site VPN, Azure ExpressRoute and Google Cloud Router all bring up BGP sessions with your routers. Their limits are low. AWS, for example, accepts at most 100 prefixes from you on a Direct Connect private virtual interface and drops the session to idle beyond that, so aggregate first. Bring-your-own-IP works through RPKI: you publish a ROA authorising the cloud's ASN, and the cloud announces your prefix.

The internet edge in 2026

  • RPKI ROV is the norm at large networks. Most large transit providers and many big access networks drop Invalids, and more than half of announced IPv4 prefixes are covered by ROAs. (NIST's RPKI Monitor and the MANRS observatory track the live numbers.) The practical consequence for you: a wrong or missing ROA is now an outage risk, not a hygiene item.
  • Leak prevention is moving into the protocol. RFC 9234 roles and the OTC attribute are implemented in BIRD, FRR and some commercial stacks, and ASPA is in standardisation with early validator and router support.
  • Anycast carries QUIC. HTTP/3 runs over UDP to anycast addresses, and a route change mid-connection can send a connection's packets to a PoP that has no state for it. QUIC's connection IDs let a large CDN route such packets internally to the right server, but only if the provider built for it. The BGP-level problem is the same one TCP always had.
  • Observability is public. bgp.tools, RIPE RIS Live (a real-time stream of BGP updates), Cloudflare Radar and RouteViews let anyone watch the global table change. Route monitoring for your own prefixes is a cheap, standard alert to have.

Deep dive: senior interview questions

Q1: Explain why MED can cause route oscillation, and what deterministic-med changes.

MED is only comparable between paths from the same neighbouring AS, so the "better than" relation over all paths is not transitive (§9, scenario 4). If a router compares paths pairwise in arrival order, its choice depends on that order. With route reflectors, which only pass on their own best path, different routers can see different subsets of paths and make choices that keep invalidating each other, so the AS oscillates forever (RFC 3345). deterministic-med groups paths by neighbour AS, picks the best within each group using MED, then compares the group winners without MED. That makes the result independent of arrival order. Add-path, which lets reflectors advertise more than one path, removes the hidden-path half of the problem.

Q2: Your prefix is being hijacked right now with a more-specific. Walk me through the first fifteen minutes.

Confirm and scope it: a looking glass and bgp.tools show the bogus prefix, its origin AS and the upstream carrying it. RIPEstat or your validator shows whether ROV networks already see it as Invalid. Then counter-announce the same more-specific yourself, if your ROA permits it, so the two announcements compete at equal length and ordinary best-path selection wins some networks back to you. A /24 is the floor in IPv4, since /25s won't propagate. In parallel, contact the offending AS's upstreams (PeeringDB and the RIR records give the NOC contacts) and ask them to filter. Afterwards: fix your ROA so the hijack is Invalid, and add route monitoring if you didn't have it.

Q3: Why does BGP use TCP, and what problems does that cause?

TCP gives BGP reliable, ordered delivery, so BGP can be incremental. It sends each route once and assumes the peer has it until it says otherwise, with no periodic refresh. The cost is that BGP inherits TCP's failure modes. Head-of-line blocking means a stuck segment stalls everything, including KEEPALIVEs (the MTU failure above). TCP's own retransmission timers delay failure detection, which is one reason BFD exists. And TCP is attackable: a spoofed RST can tear down a session. That's why GTSM (TTL 255) and TCP-AO (RFC 5925, replacing TCP MD5 from RFC 2385) are used on exposed sessions.

Q4: Why do large data centres run eBGP rather than OSPF in the fabric?

Failure scope, simplicity and scale. A link-state IGP floods every topology change to every router in an area and reruns SPF everywhere, so one flapping link costs the whole fabric CPU, and one bug can take down the whole flooding domain. eBGP keeps state per session and per prefix, and only advertises best paths. AS_PATH loop detection with shared spine ASNs gives loop-free multipath with no extra machinery. The policy tools are rich enough to drain a device for maintenance by prepending or by the graceful-shutdown community. RFC 7938 describes the design; BGP unnumbered removes the address-planning chore.

Q5: An anycast DNS service is up, but users in one country can't resolve. How do you debug it?

Find out which site those users actually reach. Many anycast DNS services answer dig CH TXT id.server or hostname.bind with a site identifier. Check from a vantage point in that country (a RIPE Atlas probe, a looking glass) what path its networks use to the anycast prefix. Common causes: a site that is up for BGP but broken behind (it should withdraw and didn't); a local network that accepts a more-specific or a leaked path to some distant site; or ROV dropping a site's announcement because one site uses a different origin ASN not in the ROA. Fix the health-check-to-withdrawal link, and keep the same origin ASN and ROA coverage across all sites.

Q6: A route is in the BGP table but packets don't follow it. List the places it can be lost.

Between Adj-RIB-In and forwarding there are five gates. Import policy can filter it (it shows only with soft-reconfiguration or as received-only). It can lose best-path to another path for the same prefix. Its next hop can be unresolvable through the RIB, making it ineligible. It can win in BGP but lose in the RIB to a route with lower administrative distance. Or it can fail to program into the FIB because of a full TCAM or a netlink error. And even when installed, a more-specific route from any source wins for the addresses it covers. Check show bgp, show ip route, and the hardware or kernel FIB in that order.

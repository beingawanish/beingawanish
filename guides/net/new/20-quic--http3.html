Module 20 — QUIC & HTTP/3

Networking Mastery Guide · Module 20 of 23

1. Why it matters

Open the developer tools in any browser, load a large site, and add the "Protocol" column. A good share of the rows say h3. Those requests never touched TCP. They rode on UDP datagrams to port 443, inside a transport that lives in the browser and in the CDN's server process rather than in either kernel.

That is a bigger change than the version number suggests. For thirty years, "reliable transport" meant TCP, and TCP meant the kernel. Your firewall rules, load balancers, packet captures, tuning knobs (net.ipv4.tcp_*), and mental models all assumed it. QUIC moves reliability, congestion control, encryption and multiplexing into a user-space library that speaks UDP. It encrypts almost everything a middlebox used to read.

So the questions you get paged for change shape. "Why is this user on HTTP/2 when we enabled HTTP/3?" "Why do QUIC connections die every time a phone walks out of Wi-Fi range — and only behind our new load balancer?" "Why did CPU on the edge fleet go up 30% when we turned it on?" None of these are answered by ss -ti.

What this module covers: why TCP plus TLS hit a wall (head-of-line blocking and ossification), QUIC's packets, frames and streams, connection IDs and migration, the 1-RTT and 0-RTT handshakes with TLS 1.3 built in, loss recovery and congestion control in user space, QPACK, how clients discover HTTP/3 (Alt-Svc and HTTPS DNS records), what happens when UDP is blocked, and the operational realities: CPU cost, middleboxes, load balancing by connection ID, and debugging with qlog and curl --http3. The standards are RFC 9000 (QUIC transport), RFC 9001 (QUIC with TLS), RFC 9002 (loss detection and congestion control), RFC 9114 (HTTP/3) and RFC 9204 (QPACK).

2. The mental model

Module 10 described TCP as a reliable, ordered byte stream. Module 12 put TLS on top of it and HTTP on top of that. Each layer was designed separately, by different people, decades apart. Each one does its own handshake.

QUIC collapses the transport and the security layer into one protocol. Hold this picture:

QUIC is a set of independent, reliable byte streams, multiplexed inside one encrypted connection, carried in UDP datagrams, and identified by connection IDs instead of by IP addresses and ports.

Every phrase in that sentence replaces something TCP did differently.

A set of independent streams. TCP gives you exactly one ordered stream per connection. HTTP/2 built many logical streams on top of it, but they all share TCP's single sequence space underneath. Lose one packet and every stream waits. QUIC gives each stream its own ordering, so a loss stalls only the stream whose bytes were in the lost packet.

Inside one encrypted connection. The TLS 1.3 handshake is not a separate layer that starts after the transport is up. QUIC carries the TLS handshake messages in its own frames, and the keys TLS produces protect QUIC's packets directly. Transport setup and crypto setup happen in the same round trip.

Carried in UDP datagrams. UDP is the thinnest thing you can put on IP and still get through the Internet. It gives QUIC port numbers and a checksum, nothing else. Everything interesting happens in user space. That is why QUIC can evolve at the speed of a browser release instead of the speed of a kernel upgrade.

Identified by connection IDs. A TCP connection is its 4-tuple: source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port. Change any of them and the connection is gone. A QUIC connection is identified by opaque connection IDs the endpoints choose. When a phone moves from Wi-Fi to cellular, its address changes, but the server still recognises the packets. The connection survives.

And one property sits underneath all four: almost everything is encrypted, including most of the header. The network can see that a packet is QUIC, which connection ID it carries, and (for handshake packets) the version. It cannot see packet numbers, acknowledgements, stream IDs or window sizes. That was a deliberate choice, and it is the answer to ossification, which section 3 explains.

3. How it works

Why TCP plus TLS hit a wall

Two problems pushed the industry off TCP. Neither could be fixed inside TCP.

Head-of-line blocking. HTTP/2 multiplexes many requests over one TCP connection. That fixed HTTP/1.1's problem of one-request-at-a-time per connection. But TCP delivers bytes to the application strictly in order. If segment 7 is lost and segments 8 through 40 arrive, the kernel holds all 33 of them in the receive buffer until the retransmission of 7 lands. It does this even when segments 8 through 40 belong to completely different HTTP/2 streams, say a CSS file and three images that have nothing to do with the lost bytes.

On a clean network that costs little. On a lossy one, such as mobile or congested Wi-Fi, it is brutal. A single loss freezes every resource on the page for at least one round trip. With 2% loss, HTTP/2's one connection can do worse than HTTP/1.1's six parallel connections, because six connections at least lose independently. The m20_hol_sim.py model in section 9 shows the effect with one lost packet: ten of twelve packets sit stuck behind it under TCP, three under QUIC.

Ossification. In principle, you fix TCP by changing TCP. In practice, the Internet won't let you. Firewalls, NATs, load balancers and "TCP accelerators" parse TCP headers. Many of them drop or rewrite packets with options or behaviours they don't recognise. Two real examples:

  • TCP Fast Open (RFC 7413) lets a client send data in the SYN, saving a round trip. It was standardised in 2014. Middleboxes that drop SYNs carrying data, or strip the option, made it unreliable enough that browsers stopped using it.
  • TLS 1.3 itself had to disguise itself as TLS 1.2. Its records carry the legacy version 0x0303, and its "middlebox compatibility mode" sends a fake ChangeCipherSpec message — because during deployment testing, a measurable fraction of connections broke when the handshake looked new.

Add the fact that TCP lives in the kernel. A change needs an OS update on billions of devices, some of which never get updates. The protocol is effectively frozen.

QUIC answers both. Streams are independent at the transport, so no cross-stream head-of-line blocking. And the transport lives in user space and encrypts its own control information. Middleboxes cannot ossify what they cannot read, and a browser can ship a new congestion controller next month.

Packets, frames and streams

QUIC has three layers of structure, and confusing them is the most common mistake people make reading a qlog.

A UDP datagram carries one or more QUIC packets (several packets in one datagram is called coalescing; it happens during the handshake). A packet has a header, a packet number, and an encrypted payload. The payload is a sequence of frames. Frames are where the work happens: STREAM frames carry application bytes, ACK frames acknowledge packets, CRYPTO frames carry TLS handshake messages, MAX_DATA frames grant flow-control credit, and so on.

This split is what makes QUIC's reliability different from TCP's. Packets are never retransmitted. Frames are. When a packet is declared lost, the sender looks at which frames it carried, decides which ones still matter, and puts them into new packets with new packet numbers. A lost ACK frame is usually not resent at all, because a fresher ACK will go out anyway. A lost STREAM frame's bytes are resent, possibly split differently, possibly mixed with other streams' data.

Packet numbers only ever go up, so there is never a question of whether an ACK is for the original or the retransmission. TCP has exactly that ambiguity (Karn's problem), which is why it needs timestamps or must discard RTT samples from retransmitted segments. QUIC gets clean RTT samples for free.

Streams come in two kinds and two owners. The low two bits of the 62-bit stream ID say which:

Low bits Meaning HTTP/3 uses it for
0x0 client-initiated, bidirectional every request/response pair (stream 0, 4, 8, …)
0x1 server-initiated, bidirectional not used by HTTP/3
0x2 client-initiated, unidirectional client control stream, QPACK encoder/decoder streams
0x3 server-initiated, unidirectional server control stream, QPACK streams, push

Opening a stream needs no handshake. The sender just uses the next ID, up to the limit the peer granted with MAX_STREAMS. A stream's bytes carry offsets, so the receiver can reassemble them in order no matter how they arrive. Each stream can be ended cleanly (the FIN bit on its last STREAM frame) or aborted (RESET_STREAM from the sender, STOP_SENDING from the receiver) without touching any other stream.

Connection IDs and migration

Every QUIC packet carries a Destination Connection ID (DCID). Each endpoint picks the IDs it wants to receive, and the peer puts them in the DCID of packets it sends. So the client sends to the server's chosen ID and the server sends to the client's chosen ID. IDs are 0 to 20 bytes long in QUIC version 1.

The server's job when a datagram arrives is: read the DCID, find the connection object with that ID, hand it the packet. The source address is secondary. That single lookup is what makes connection migration possible.

Two different events change a client's address:

  • NAT rebinding. The client didn't move, but its NAT forgot the mapping (usually after an idle timeout) and picked a new external port. The client doesn't even know. The server just sees the same DCID from a new address.
  • Intentional migration. The phone switches from Wi-Fi to cellular. The client knows, and it deliberately starts sending from the new interface.

In both cases the server sees a known DCID from an unknown address. It does not trust the new address blindly. An attacker could spoof a source address to aim traffic at a victim. So the server sends a PATH_CHALLENGE frame carrying 8 random bytes to the new address. The client echoes them in PATH_RESPONSE. Until the path validates, the server limits what it sends there. After it validates, the server resets its congestion controller for the new path, because the old path's window says nothing about the new one.

There is a privacy wrinkle. If the client kept the same connection ID across networks, an observer on both networks could link them. So each endpoint hands its peer a pool of spare IDs in NEW_CONNECTION_ID frames, and a client that migrates on purpose switches to an unused ID at the same time. Only the endpoints know the IDs belong to one connection.

Only the client migrates. Servers stay put (with one narrow exception, the preferred_address transport parameter, which lets a server ask clients to move once after the handshake). And a server that can't handle migration — say, because its load balancer hashes on the 4-tuple — can send the disable_active_migration transport parameter.

The handshake: TLS 1.3 inside the transport

Module 12 showed TLS 1.3 needing one round trip after TCP's own one round trip: two RTTs before the first HTTP request leaves the client. QUIC does both jobs in a single round trip.

It works because QUIC does not use TLS records at all. TLS still runs the handshake state machine: ClientHello, ServerHello, certificate, Finished. But instead of wrapping those messages in TLS records and writing them to a TCP socket, the TLS library hands the raw handshake bytes to QUIC. QUIC puts them in CRYPTO frames. When TLS derives a new key, it hands the key to QUIC, and QUIC uses it to protect packets.

QUIC's own parameters ride inside the TLS handshake too, in a TLS extension called quic_transport_parameters. That is how each side learns the other's flow-control limits, idle timeout, maximum packet size, and connection-ID limits — and because the extension is part of the signed handshake transcript, an attacker cannot tamper with them.

There are four encryption levels, each with its own keys:

  1. Initial — keys derived from the client's first DCID and a fixed, public salt (RFC 9001 §5.2). Anyone who sees the first packet can derive them. They stop off-path attackers from injecting junk and stop middleboxes from casually parsing the handshake. They are not confidentiality.
  2. 0-RTT — keys from a previous session's resumption secret. Only on resumption, only from the client.
  3. Handshake — keys from the (EC)DHE exchange. The certificate is encrypted under these.
  4. 1-RTT — the application keys, used for everything after the handshake.

Packets are also grouped into three packet number spaces: Initial, Handshake, and Application Data (0-RTT and 1-RTT share this one). Each space has its own packet numbers starting at 0 and its own ACKs. An ACK in a Handshake packet can only acknowledge Handshake packets. This keeps the key changes clean: when an endpoint discards Initial keys, it discards the whole Initial space with them.

Walk through the diagram.

Client flight (t = 0). The client picks a random DCID — at least 8 bytes — and derives Initial keys from it. It sends an Initial packet holding a CRYPTO frame with the ClientHello. The datagram must be padded to at least 1,200 bytes. That rule has two purposes: it proves the path can carry 1,200-byte datagrams, which QUIC requires, and it buys the server an amplification budget.

Server flight (t = ½ RTT at the server). Until the server has seen proof that the client really owns its source address, it may send at most three times the bytes it has received from that address (RFC 9000 §8.1). The padded 1,200-byte Initial gives it 3,600 bytes. Into that budget it packs an Initial packet (ServerHello and an ACK) and Handshake packets (EncryptedExtensions, Certificate, CertificateVerify, Finished), usually coalesced into the first datagram and spilling into one or two more.

Client again (t = 1 RTT). The client now holds Handshake and 1-RTT keys. It sends its Finished in a Handshake packet. In the same datagram or the next one it sends a 1-RTT packet carrying the HTTP request. The request leaves the client one round trip after the first packet. With TCP plus TLS 1.3 it leaves after two.

Server confirms. When the server receives the client's Finished, the handshake is complete. It sends a HANDSHAKE_DONE frame, and both sides throw away their Handshake keys. The response follows on the request's stream.

Retry: the server's escape hatch. Under load or attack, a server may not want to allocate any state, not even the 3× budget, for an address it hasn't validated. It can answer the first Initial with a Retry packet containing a token. The client repeats its Initial with the token, which proves it can receive at that address. It costs a full round trip, so servers use it when under pressure rather than by default. NEW_TOKEN frames let a server hand clients a token for their next connection, so a returning client can skip validation.

0-RTT and what it costs

A client that connected before holds a session ticket and remembers the server's transport parameters. On the next connection it can send 0-RTT packets — long-header type 0x1 — in the same flight as its ClientHello. The HTTP request reaches the server half a round trip after the client starts, and the response comes back after one RTT total.

The cost is the same as Module 12's TLS 0-RTT: early data can be replayed. The server cannot yet prove the packet is fresh. An attacker who captured the client's first flight can resend it, and the server may process the request twice. So the rules are:

  • Only send requests that are safe to replay. Browsers send GET in 0-RTT, never POST.
  • A server or proxy that is unsure can answer 425 Too Early (RFC 8470), and the client retries after the handshake.
  • A proxy that forwards early data to an origin adds the Early-Data: 1 header so the origin can make that decision itself.
  • A server may simply reject 0-RTT. The client then resends everything in 1-RTT packets. Nothing breaks; the client only loses the time saved.

Loss recovery and congestion control in user space

RFC 9002 describes how QUIC detects loss. The ideas are TCP's, cleaned up.

A packet is declared lost when a packet sent after it has been acknowledged and either of two thresholds is crossed:

  • Packet threshold: three later packets have been acknowledged (kPacketThreshold = 3, the same intuition as TCP's three duplicate ACKs).
  • Time threshold: it has been outstanding for more than 9/8 of the larger of the smoothed RTT and the latest RTT sample.

If nothing comes back at all — the tail of a transfer was lost, or the ACKs were — the probe timeout (PTO) fires. The sender then sends one or two probe packets to elicit an ACK. PTO replaces TCP's retransmission timeout, with one important difference: a PTO firing does not collapse the congestion window. Only real, confirmed loss does.

ACKs are richer than TCP's. A QUIC ACK frame lists up to hundreds of acknowledged ranges, like TCP SACK without the 3–4 block limit of the TCP options field. It also reports the ACK delay: how long the receiver deliberately held the ACK. The sender subtracts it from the RTT sample, so delayed ACKs don't inflate the RTT estimate.

For congestion control, RFC 9002 specifies a NewReno-style controller as a baseline, but implementations are free to use anything. Common choices are CUBIC and BBR. Because the controller is a library, a CDN can A/B test a new algorithm on 1% of connections and roll it back the same day. With TCP that requires a kernel change on the server and does nothing for the client's sending direction.

Flow control works at two levels at once: MAX_STREAM_DATA limits each stream, and MAX_DATA limits the whole connection. A third limit, MAX_STREAMS, caps how many streams the peer may open. All three start from transport parameters and are raised by frames as the application consumes data.

HTTP/3 and QPACK

HTTP/3 (RFC 9114) is HTTP's semantics — methods, status codes, headers, Module 12 unchanged — mapped onto QUIC streams.

Each request gets its own client-initiated bidirectional stream. The client writes a HEADERS frame, optionally DATA frames for a body, and closes its side. The server answers with HEADERS, DATA, maybe trailing HEADERS, and closes its side. There is no request ID in the frames; the stream is the request. Cancelling a request is a RESET_STREAM or STOP_SENDING on that stream.

Each side also opens a unidirectional control stream (stream type 0x00) and sends SETTINGS first. GOAWAY travels there too, for graceful shutdown. Several jobs HTTP/2 did in its own framing layer have moved down into QUIC: flow control, stream limits, and stream reset. HTTP/3 doesn't duplicate them.

Prioritisation in HTTP/3 uses Extensible Priorities (RFC 9218): a priority header such as priority: u=1, i (urgency 0–7, plus "incremental") and a PRIORITY_UPDATE frame for changes. HTTP/2's dependency tree is gone. Server push exists in the spec, but browsers have effectively abandoned it; don't build on it.

QPACK (RFC 9204) is header compression. HTTP/2's HPACK keeps a dynamic table of recent headers that both sides update in lock-step as header blocks are processed. That works because TCP processes header blocks in order. On QUIC, the HEADERS for stream 8 can arrive before the HEADERS for stream 4. If stream 8's headers refer to a table entry that stream 4's headers were supposed to add, the decoder is stuck — head-of-line blocking again, reintroduced by the compressor.

QPACK's fix is to take table updates off the request streams:

  • The encoder stream (unidirectional, type 0x02) carries instructions that insert entries into the dynamic table.
  • Each header block starts with a Required Insert Count: "you need at least this many insertions before you can decode me."
  • If the decoder hasn't received those insertions yet, that one stream blocks. Others continue. SETTINGS_QPACK_BLOCKED_STREAMS caps how many streams the encoder may put at risk.
  • The decoder stream (type 0x03) carries acknowledgements back, so the encoder knows which entries are safe to reference without any blocking risk.

The static table has 99 entries (HPACK's had 61), chosen from real traffic. :method GET is index 17, :scheme https is 23, :status 200 is 25. A server that sets the dynamic table capacity to 0 — the default — uses only the static table and literals, and never blocks. Many deployments run exactly that way, trading a little compression for simplicity.

Discovery: how a client knows to try HTTP/3

A URL says https://. It doesn't say which protocol. A client has three ways to learn that a server speaks HTTP/3.

Alt-Svc (RFC 7838). The classic path. The client connects over TCP, and the response carries a header like:

alt-svc: h3=":443"; ma=86400

That says: "the same origin is also available over HTTP/3 on UDP port 443, and you may remember this for 86,400 seconds." The client caches it per origin. Its next connection tries QUIC. The very first visit pays for TCP.

The HTTPS DNS record (RFC 9460). A DNS record type (HTTPS, type 65) that publishes connection hints before any connection exists:

example.com. 300 IN HTTPS 1 . alpn="h3,h2" ipv4hint=192.0.2.10 ipv6hint=2001:db8::10

alpn="h3,h2" tells the client it can try HTTP/3 on the first connection. The same record can carry an ech parameter with the keys for Encrypted Client Hello. Browser support depends on the browser, the OS, and which resolver path is in use, so treat it as an accelerator, not a guarantee.

Configuration. Clients told explicitly — curl --http3, an internal service mesh, an app that hard-codes it.

UDP blocking and fallback

Some networks block UDP 443. Enterprise firewalls do it on purpose, so all web traffic passes through their TCP inspection proxy. Some hotel and guest networks do it by accident, allowing only DNS on UDP. Chrome even has an enterprise policy, QuicAllowed, so administrators can turn QUIC off.

A browser can't afford to hang when that happens. So in practice browsers race: when they have an h3 hint, they start the QUIC handshake and, after a short delay or in parallel, a TCP+TLS handshake. Whichever completes first wins. If QUIC fails, the browser marks HTTP/3 as broken for that origin for some minutes and uses TCP. Users on a UDP-blocking network get HTTP/2, a little slower, and never notice.

The ugly case is a silent drop with a client that doesn't race. A CLI with --http3-only or an app that insists on QUIC waits for its handshake timeout. Then it either errors or falls back late. The rule for any service you run: HTTP/3 must always be an upgrade, never the only path. Keep TCP listening on the same name.

4. The exact structure

Long header (Initial, 0-RTT, Handshake, Retry)

Long headers are used until the handshake completes. Their layout is partly fixed forever by RFC 8999 ("Version-Independent Properties of QUIC"): the first bit, the version, and the two connection IDs with their lengths. Everything else may change in future versions.

Field Size Notes
Header Form 1 bit 1 = long header
Fixed Bit 1 bit 1 in v1 (RFC 9287 lets peers agree to randomise it — "greasing")
Long Packet Type 2 bits 00 Initial, 01 0-RTT, 10 Handshake, 11 Retry
Type-specific bits 4 bits 2 reserved + 2 for packet-number length (protected)
Version 32 bits 0x00000001 = QUIC v1; 0x6b3343cf = QUIC v2 (RFC 9369); 0 = Version Negotiation
DCID Length + DCID 8 bits + 0–20 bytes the ID the receiver chose
SCID Length + SCID 8 bits + 0–20 bytes the ID the sender wants to receive

The Length field is what makes coalescing possible: the receiver knows where this packet ends and the next one in the datagram begins.

Short header (1-RTT)

After the handshake, every packet uses the short header. It is tiny.

Field Size Notes
Header Form 1 bit 0 = short header
Fixed Bit 1 bit 1
Spin Bit 1 bit optional passive RTT signal; may be disabled
Reserved 2 bits protected
Key Phase 1 bit flips on each key update (protected)
Packet Number Length 2 bits protected
Destination Connection ID 0–20 bytes no length field — the receiver knows its own CID length
Packet Number 1–4 bytes protected
Payload rest encrypted frames + 16-byte tag

Note what isn't there: no source CID, no version, no length. A short-header packet must be the last one in its datagram. And a load balancer that wants to read the DCID must know how long the server's CIDs are, because the packet doesn't say. Section "Deep dive: production failure modes" comes back to this.

Header protection. After the payload is encrypted, the sender takes a 16-byte sample of the ciphertext (starting 4 bytes after the packet number begins), encrypts it with a separate header-protection key, and XORs the result over the packet number bytes and the low bits of the first byte. An observer therefore can't read packet numbers or the key phase. This is what keeps middleboxes from building loss or RTT heuristics on QUIC's internals, the way they did with TCP sequence numbers.

Variable-length integers

Almost every number on the wire — stream IDs, offsets, lengths, frame types, error codes — is a varint (RFC 9000 §16). The top two bits of the first byte give the length:

00 1 byte 6 63
01 2 bytes 14 16,383
10 4 bytes 30 1,073,741,823
11 8 bytes 62 4,611,686,018,427,387,903

That is why stream IDs and packet numbers are "62-bit". Section 9's m20_wire.py encodes and decodes these, checked against RFC 9000's test vectors.

Read it in order.

Frame 1 is the client's first flight. The frame is 1,242 bytes on Ethernet: 14 (Ethernet) + 20 (IPv4) + 8 (UDP) + 1,200 (QUIC). The ClientHello itself is a few hundred bytes. The rest is PADDING, there only to meet the 1,200-byte minimum. The DCID c1a5e0f39b2d4471 is random; the client made it up. The SCID 0b6e9d1f7c2a5e10 is the ID the client wants the server to use when replying.

Frames 2–4 are the server's flight, all sent within 20 µs. Look at the DCIDs: the server now addresses the client by 0b6e…, and offers its own ID 5ad3e1040c9f22b7 as SCID. Frame 2 coalesces an Initial packet (ServerHello plus an ACK of the client's PKN 0) with the first Handshake packet. The three datagrams carry 1,200 + 1,200 + 734 = 3,134 bytes of QUIC. That is under the 3 × 1,200 = 3,600-byte amplification limit, so the whole certificate chain fits in the first flight.

Notice that the packet numbers restart. The server's Initial is PKN 0 and its first Handshake is also PKN 0. Different spaces, different counters.

Frame 5 is the client completing the handshake: an Initial packet that just ACKs the server's Initial, plus a Handshake packet with an ACK and the client's Finished in a CRYPTO frame. Because it contains an Initial packet, the datagram is padded to 1,200 bytes again. The client's Initial keys are still the ones derived from c1a5…, the original DCID, even though the client now addresses the server as 5ad3….

Frame 6 is 11 µs later, and it is the payoff: a short-header 1-RTT packet carrying the HTTP/3 request on stream 0 at t = 21 ms, one round trip after the first packet. Streams 2, 6 and 10 are the client's three unidirectional streams: control (with SETTINGS), QPACK encoder and QPACK decoder. "Protected Payload (KP0)" is how tshark labels a short-header packet; KP0 means key phase 0, the first set of 1-RTT keys.

Frame 7 arrives one RTT after frame 6: HANDSHAKE_DONE (the server has the client's Finished; both sides drop Handshake keys), a NEW_TOKEN for the client's next visit, a spare connection ID, and the server's control stream (stream 3, server-initiated unidirectional) with its SETTINGS. Frame 8 is the response on stream 0, and frame 9 acknowledges it.

Two things about this capture are worth carrying with you.

First, without the key log tshark still decodes frames 1, 2 and 5 completely. Initial keys come from the DCID, which is in the packet. Everything else shows as encrypted. This is the honest way to describe Initial protection: integrity against off-path attackers, not secrecy.

Second, the server's first datagram is the same size as the client's. A server that sent a 5 KB certificate chain would hit the 3,600-byte limit. It would then have to wait for frame 5's ACK before sending the rest — an extra round trip that shows up nowhere unless you look for it. "Deep dive: production failure modes" covers it.

6. Misconceptions & common mistakes

"QUIC is UDP, so it's unreliable." QUIC uses UDP the way TCP uses IP. It adds acknowledgements, retransmission, ordering per stream, flow control and congestion control. It is every bit as reliable as TCP. The optional unreliable extension (DATAGRAM frames, RFC 9221) is a separate, explicit feature.

"HTTP/3 removes head-of-line blocking completely." It removes cross-stream blocking at the transport. Within one stream, bytes are still delivered in order, so a loss still stalls that stream. And a QPACK decoder can block a stream waiting for a dynamic-table insertion, which is why SETTINGS_QPACK_BLOCKED_STREAMS exists.

"QUIC is always faster." On a clean, low-latency wired path, HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 perform about the same, and HTTP/3 can be slower if the stack is CPU-bound or has small socket buffers. The wins are on the handshake (one RTT saved, more with 0-RTT), on lossy paths (no cross-stream blocking), and on networks where addresses change (migration).

"0-RTT is just a faster handshake." It is a faster handshake with replayable data. Treat it the way Module 12 did: off by default for anything non-idempotent, 425 Too Early when unsure.

"Encryption means firewalls can't see QUIC at all." They can see a lot: UDP port 443, the version and both connection IDs in long headers, the DCID in short headers, packet sizes and timing, and — because Initial keys are derivable — the full ClientHello, including SNI (unless Encrypted Client Hello is used). What they cannot see is the transport's internals: packet numbers, ACKs, windows, stream IDs.

"A connection ID is a session ID I can log and track users with." Endpoints rotate CIDs on purpose and switch CIDs on migration so that observers can't link paths. Server-side, a CID is a routing key. It is not a user identifier.

"If UDP 443 is blocked, HTTP/3 sites break." Browsers race or fall back to TCP, so the site works over HTTP/2. Sites break only if someone deploys HTTP/3 without keeping TCP, or if a client insists on QUIC.

"Enabling HTTP/3 on the server is enough." The server also needs UDP 443 open in every security group, firewall and load balancer on the path, and an Alt-Svc header or HTTPS record that clients actually receive. A missing UDP listener is the single most common reason "we enabled HTTP/3" shows zero h3 traffic.

7. Interview Q&A

Q1: HTTP/2 already multiplexes streams. What problem does HTTP/3 solve that HTTP/2 couldn't?

A: HTTP/2's streams are multiplexed above TCP, and TCP has one sequence space. A single lost segment stops the kernel from handing any later bytes to the application, whichever stream they belong to. That is transport-level head-of-line blocking. HTTP/2 can't fix it, because the ordering is enforced in the kernel below it. QUIC moves streams into the transport, each with its own ordering, so a loss only stalls the stream whose data was lost. HTTP/3 is HTTP mapped onto those streams. It also gets a one-RTT combined handshake and connection migration.

Q2: Why is QUIC built on UDP instead of being a new IP protocol number, like SCTP?

A: Deployability. NATs and firewalls pass TCP and UDP and very little else. SCTP is a fine protocol that almost never works across the Internet for exactly this reason. UDP is the minimal header that gets through, and running in user space means QUIC can ship and evolve with applications instead of waiting for kernel updates on every device.

Q3: How does a QUIC connection survive a client changing IP address?

A: The server identifies the connection by the Destination Connection ID in each packet, not the 4-tuple. When a known DCID arrives from a new address, the server validates the new path with PATH_CHALLENGE/PATH_RESPONSE, resets congestion state for it, and continues. A client that migrates deliberately also switches to a fresh CID from the pool its peer gave it, so observers can't link the two paths.

Q4: How does QUIC get to one round trip when TCP plus TLS 1.3 needs two?

A: It merges the transport and crypto handshakes. The TLS 1.3 handshake messages ride in QUIC CRYPTO frames from the very first packet, and QUIC's transport parameters ride inside the TLS handshake. There is no separate transport-only exchange like TCP's SYN/SYN-ACK. The client can send its request in a 1-RTT packet together with its Finished.

Q5: Why must the client pad its first Initial packet to 1,200 bytes?

A: Two reasons. It proves the path carries datagrams of QUIC's minimum size. And it bounds amplification: before a server validates the client's address, it may send at most three times what it received. A 1,200-byte Initial lets the server send 3,600 bytes. Without padding, a spoofed 300-byte Initial could make the server blast a large certificate chain at a victim.

Q6: You're asked to deploy HTTP/3. What has to be true end to end?

A: A QUIC-capable server or edge proxy listening on UDP 443. UDP 443 allowed through every firewall, security group, and load balancer on the path. A load balancer that either terminates QUIC or routes UDP by connection ID, not just by 4-tuple. Discovery: an Alt-Svc: h3=":443" header on TCP responses, and optionally an HTTPS DNS record with alpn="h3,h2". TCP+TLS still available on the same name for clients that can't use UDP. And monitoring that splits metrics by protocol, so you can see whether h3 traffic is real and healthy.

"""QUIC wire basics: variable-length integers (RFC 9000 §16) and a long-header parser.

Test vectors come from RFC 9000 Appendix A.1; the packet header is the
unprotected client Initial from RFC 9001 Appendix A.2.
"""


def encode_varint(v: int) -> bytes:
    for prefix, length in ((0b00, 1), (0b01, 2), (0b10, 4), (0b11, 8)):
        if v < 1 << (8 * length - 2):
            return ((prefix << (8 * length - 2)) | v).to_bytes(length, "big")
    raise ValueError("varint max is 2**62 - 1")


def decode_varint(buf: bytes, pos: int = 0) -> tuple[int, int]:
    length = 1 << (buf[pos] >> 6)               # top two bits: 1, 2, 4 or 8 bytes
    value = buf[pos] & 0x3F
    for b in buf[pos + 1 : pos + length]:
        value = (value << 8) | b
    return value, pos + length


PACKET_TYPES = {0: "Initial", 1: "0-RTT", 2: "Handshake", 3: "Retry"}


def parse_long_header(pkt: bytes) -> list[tuple[str, str]]:
    first = pkt[0]
    out = [("first byte", f"0x{first:02x} = {first:08b}"),
           ("  header form (bit 7)", "1 = long header" if first & 0x80 else "0 = short header"),
           ("  fixed bit (bit 6)", str((first >> 6) & 1)),
           ("  packet type (bits 5-4)", PACKET_TYPES[(first >> 4) & 3]),
           ("  reserved (bits 3-2)", str((first >> 2) & 3)),
           ("  pn length (bits 1-0)", f"{(first & 3) + 1} bytes")]
    pos = 1
    out.append(("version", "0x" + pkt[pos : pos + 4].hex())); pos += 4
    dl = pkt[pos]; pos += 1
    out.append((f"DCID ({dl} bytes)", pkt[pos : pos + dl].hex())); pos += dl
    sl = pkt[pos]; pos += 1

    out.append(("token length", str(tok_len))); pos += tok_len
    start = pos

    out.append((f"length (varint {pkt[start:pos].hex()})", f"{length} bytes follow (pn + payload + 16-byte tag)"))

    out.append(("packet number", str(int.from_bytes(pkt[pos : pos + pn_len], "big"))))
    out.append(("header size", f"{pos + pn_len} bytes"))
    out.append(("datagram size", f"{pos + length} bytes"))

    """Encode GET https://<authority>/ using only the static table (no dynamic state)."""
    out = bytes([0x00, 0x00])                     # Required Insert Count 0, Delta Base 0
    for idx in (17, 23, 1):                       # Indexed Field Line: 1 T=1 index(6)

    return out

    return encode_varint(ftype) + encode_varint(stream_id) + encode_varint(len(data)) + data

Initial keys anyone can derive

code/m20/m20_initial_keys.py implements HKDF with hmac and hashlib and derives the Initial keys from a DCID, exactly as RFC 9001 §5.2 specifies.

"""Derive QUIC v1 Initial keys from a Destination Connection ID (RFC 9001 §5.2).

Stdlib only: HKDF-Extract / HKDF-Expand-Label are a few lines of hmac + hashlib.
The DCID below is the one used in RFC 9001 Appendix A, so the output can be
checked against the RFC's published test vectors.
"""
import hashlib
import hmac

INITIAL_SALT_V1 = bytes.fromhex("38762cf7f55934b34d179ae6a4c80cadccbb7f0a")


def hkdf_extract(salt: bytes, ikm: bytes) -> bytes:
    return hmac.new(salt, ikm, hashlib.sha256).digest()


def hkdf_expand_label(secret: bytes, label: str, length: int) -> bytes:
    # TLS 1.3 HkdfLabel: uint16 length, opaque label<7..255> = "tls13 " + label, context<0..255> = ""
    full = b"tls13 " + label.encode()
    info = length.to_bytes(2, "big") + bytes([len(full)]) + full + b"\x00"
    out, block, i = b"", b"", 1
    while len(out) < length:  # HKDF-Expand (RFC 5869)
        block = hmac.new(secret, block + info + bytes([i]), hashlib.sha256).digest()
        out += block
        i += 1
    return out[:length]


def initial_keys(dcid: bytes) -> dict[str, dict[str, str]]:
    initial_secret = hkdf_extract(INITIAL_SALT_V1, dcid)
    result = {}
    for side in ("client in", "server in"):
        secret = hkdf_expand_label(initial_secret, side, 32)
        result[side.split()[0]] = {
            "secret": secret.hex(),
            "key (AES-128-GCM)": hkdf_expand_label(secret, "quic key", 16).hex(),
            "iv": hkdf_expand_label(secret, "quic iv", 12).hex(),
            "hp (header protection)": hkdf_expand_label(secret, "quic hp", 16).hex(),
        }
    return result

        print(f"\n[{side} Initial]")

Head-of-line blocking, measured

code/m20/m20_hol_sim.py is a small deterministic model: three responses interleaved over one connection, one lost packet, and the same loss-detection rule (three later packets acknowledged) for both protocols. The only difference is what "in order" means.

"""Head-of-line blocking: one ordered byte stream (TCP) vs independent streams (QUIC).

A deliberately small, deterministic model. Three responses (A, B, C) of four
packets each are interleaved round-robin, one packet per millisecond, over a
path with a 20 ms one-way delay (40 ms RTT). One packet is lost. The sender
notices the loss when the ACK for the third later packet arrives (packet
threshold = 3, as in RFC 9002 and TCP's fast retransmit) and resends it at once.
"""
ONE_WAY = 20      # ms
THRESHOLD = 3     # packets
STREAMS = "ABC"
PER_STREAM = 4
LOST = 1          # index of the lost packet in send order (B1)

order = [f"{s}{n}" for n in range(1, PER_STREAM + 1) for s in STREAMS]
arrival = {p: i + ONE_WAY for i, p in enumerate(order)}
# ACK of packet LOST+THRESHOLD reaches the sender at (LOST+THRESHOLD) + 2*ONE_WAY;
# the retransmission then needs another ONE_WAY to arrive.
retx_arrival = (LOST + THRESHOLD) + 2 * ONE_WAY + ONE_WAY
arrival[order[LOST]] = retx_arrival

# TCP: the kernel hands bytes to the app strictly in send order.
tcp_deliver, horizon = {}, 0
for p in order:
    horizon = max(horizon, arrival[p])
    tcp_deliver[p] = horizon

# QUIC: each stream is ordered only against itself.
quic_deliver = {}
for s in STREAMS:
    horizon = 0
    for n in range(1, PER_STREAM + 1):
        p = f"{s}{n}"
        horizon = max(horizon, arrival[p])
        quic_deliver[p] = horizon

print(f"send order : {' '.join(order)}   (1 packet/ms, lost: {order[LOST]})")
print(f"retransmitted {order[LOST]} arrives at t={retx_arrival} ms\n")
print(f"{'pkt':<5}{'arrives':>9}{'TCP: app gets it':>18}{'QUIC: app gets it':>19}")
for p in order:
    mark = "  <- lost, resent" if p == order[LOST] else ""
    print(f"{p:<5}{arrival[p]:>7} ms{tcp_deliver[p]:>15} ms{quic_deliver[p]:>16} ms{mark}")

print("\nstream complete (last byte handed to the app):")
for s in STREAMS:
    last = f"{s}{PER_STREAM}"
    print(f"  {s}:  TCP {tcp_deliver[last]:>3} ms   QUIC {quic_deliver[last]:>3} ms")
stalled = sum(1 for p in order if tcp_deliver[p] > arrival[p])
stalled_q = sum(1 for p in order if quic_deliver[p] > arrival[p])
print(f"\npackets that sat in a receive buffer waiting for {order[LOST]}: TCP {stalled}, QUIC {stalled_q}")

Deep dive: what the overview hid

The nine sections above describe QUIC the way the RFCs present it. What they hide is where the machine actually runs and what it costs. A TCP connection is a kernel object you can list with ss. A QUIC connection is a struct inside a process, keyed by a byte string the process invented. The kernel sees one UDP socket and a stream of datagrams. That shift — from kernel state to user-space state — drives almost every operational difference that follows.

Deep dive: the real mental model

Picture a QUIC server process — an edge proxy, say — on Linux.

In the kernel there is almost nothing. One UDP socket bound to :443, or one per worker with SO_REUSEPORT. No connection table. No per-connection timers. No retransmission queue. Here is what ss shows on a server with tens of thousands of active QUIC connections (illustrative):

$ ss -uanp 'sport = :443'
State   Recv-Q  Send-Q  Local Address:Port  Peer Address:Port  Process
UNCONN  0       0             0.0.0.0:443        0.0.0.0:*      users:(("edge",pid=812,fd=9))
UNCONN  0       0             0.0.0.0:443        0.0.0.0:*      users:(("edge",pid=813,fd=9))
UNCONN  0       0                [::]:443           [::]:*      users:(("edge",pid=812,fd=10))
UNCONN  0       0                [::]:443           [::]:*      users:(("edge",pid=813,fd=10))

Four lines, whether there are ten connections or a hundred thousand. Compare ss -tan state established '( sport = :443 )' | wc -l on the same box for TCP. Every TCP connection is a kernel socket; no QUIC connection is.

In user space there is everything. Each worker holds a hash table from connection ID to connection object. Each connection object holds:

  • Per packet number space (Initial, Handshake, Application): the next packet number to send; a map of sent-but-unacknowledged packets, each remembering its send time, size, and the frames it carried; the received packet numbers as a set of ranges, to build ACK frames; the largest acknowledged; the keys for that space.
  • Connection-wide recovery state: smoothed_rtt, rttvar, min_rtt, latest_rtt; congestion_window, bytes_in_flight, ssthresh; the loss-detection timer (which doubles as the PTO timer), and the PTO backoff count.
  • Streams: a map from stream ID to a send buffer (bytes not yet acknowledged, plus which ranges are in flight), a receive buffer with an out-of-order reassembly structure, and per-stream flow-control credit both ways.
  • Connection-level flow control: bytes consumed versus MAX_DATA in each direction.
  • Identity and paths: the CIDs this side has issued (with sequence numbers and stateless-reset tokens), the CIDs the peer has issued, the current path's 4-tuple, whether it is validated, and — before validation — the anti-amplification counters: bytes received and bytes sent on that path.
  • Timers: idle timeout, the ACK delay timer, the pacing timer.

Each arriving datagram drives the machine: split it into packets; for each, read the DCID and find the connection; remove header protection; decrypt with the right space's keys; record the packet number in that space's received ranges; process each frame. An ACK frame removes packets from the sent map, produces an RTT sample, grows the congestion window, and may declare older packets lost. Their frames go back into the "to send" queues. A STREAM frame inserts bytes into a stream's reassembly buffer and wakes the reader if a contiguous prefix is ready.

The diagram's last row is the one most people forget: the load balancer in front. A stateless L4 load balancer hashes the 4-tuple to pick a backend. That works for TCP, whose 4-tuple never changes. For QUIC it works only until the client's address changes — which is exactly the case QUIC was built to survive. The fix is to route by connection ID, which requires the server to encode something routable into the CIDs it issues.

Deep dive: what the wire and the kernel actually do

At the IP layer, a QUIC packet looks like any UDP datagram. This is frame 1 from section 5 as tcpdump -nn -v would print it (constructed, consistent with section 5):

IP (tos 0x0, ttl 64, id 0, offset 0, flags [DF], proto UDP (17), length 1228)
    192.0.2.44.53122 > 198.51.100.7.443: UDP, length 1200

Three details to read. flags [DF]: RFC 9000 §14 requires QUIC to set Don't Fragment on IPv4 where it can. QUIC must never be fragmented; it finds the path MTU itself. length 1228 is 20 bytes of IP header plus 8 of UDP plus 1,200 of QUIC. And UDP, length 1200 is all tcpdump knows unless your build has a QUIC dissector — some newer builds print a short QUIC summary instead.

Path MTU without ICMP. TCP relies (poorly) on ICMP "fragmentation needed" messages to learn the path MTU. QUIC uses DPLPMTUD (RFC 8899): it sends a probe packet of PING plus PADDING at a larger size. If the probe is acknowledged, that size works. If it is lost, it doesn't, and — crucially — a lost probe is not counted as congestion. Many stacks start at 1,200–1,350 bytes and probe upward; some never probe and stay conservative.

Where the CPU goes. For TCP on Linux, the kernel does segmentation and checksums, and with TSO the NIC splits a 64 KB buffer into wire-size segments. With kTLS, even encryption can move into the kernel or NIC. A QUIC sender has to build and encrypt each ~1,200–1,450-byte packet itself, in user space, and push it across the syscall boundary. The tools that close the gap on Linux:

  • sendmmsg() / recvmmsg(): many datagrams per syscall.
  • UDP GSO (UDP_SEGMENT socket option, kernel 4.18+): hand the kernel one large buffer of equal-sized packets and let it (or the NIC) split them.
  • UDP GRO (kernel 5.0+): coalesce received datagrams of the same flow into one receive call.
  • Pacing in the stack or via SO_TXTIME, so bursts don't overflow queues.

Socket buffers. The kernel still queues datagrams between the NIC and the process. If the process falls behind, the queue overflows and packets drop before QUIC ever sees them. To QUIC, that looks exactly like network loss, and it cuts the congestion window. Linux's default receive buffer cap is small (net.core.rmem_max is commonly 208 KB). Here is quic-go's real startup warning, which tells you precisely this:

failed to sufficiently increase receive buffer size (was: 208 kiB, wanted: 7168 kiB, got: 416 kiB).
See https://github.com/quic-go/quic-go/wiki/UDP-Buffer-Sizes for details.

Prove the drops with the kernel's own counters. Linux: nstat -az | grep -E 'UdpRcvbufErrors|UdpSndbufErrors' — a rising UdpRcvbufErrors means the socket buffer overflowed. macOS: netstat -s -p udp and look for the "dropped due to full socket buffers" line. The fix on Linux is sysctl -w net.core.rmem_max=7500000 net.core.wmem_max=7500000 (persist it in /etc/sysctl.d/), and an application that actually requests the bigger buffer with SO_RCVBUF.

What a qlog shows that a pcap can't. A pcap of encrypted QUIC shows sizes and timing. A qlog is the endpoint narrating its own state: packet_sent, packet_received, packets_acked, packet_lost with the trigger (reordering_threshold, time_threshold, pto_expired), metrics_updated with congestion_window, bytes_in_flight and smoothed_rtt. An abridged, illustrative excerpt in JSON-SEQ form (the event names follow the IETF qlog drafts; exact schemas vary between implementations and draft versions):

{"time":0.0,   "name":"transport:packet_sent", "data":{"header":{"packet_type":"initial","packet_number":0},"raw":{"length":1200}}}
{"time":20.4,  "name":"transport:packet_received", "data":{"header":{"packet_type":"initial","packet_number":0}}}
{"time":20.4,  "name":"recovery:metrics_updated", "data":{"smoothed_rtt":20.4,"latest_rtt":20.4,"congestion_window":12000,"bytes_in_flight":0}}
{"time":21.1,  "name":"transport:packet_sent", "data":{"header":{"packet_type":"1RTT","packet_number":0},"frames":[{"frame_type":"stream","stream_id":0,"fin":true}]}}
{"time":412.8, "name":"recovery:packet_lost", "data":{"header":{"packet_type":"1RTT","packet_number":118},"trigger":"time_threshold"}}

The congestion_window of 12,000 is not a guess. It is RFC 9002's initial window for a 1,200-byte maximum datagram size, worked out in the next section.

Deep dive: by the numbers

Round trips to the first response byte, from a cold start (DNS already resolved):

Stack Request leaves client Response arrives At 100 ms RTT
TCP + TLS 1.2 3 RTT 4 RTT 400 ms
TCP + TLS 1.3 2 RTT 3 RTT 300 ms
QUIC (1-RTT) 1 RTT 2 RTT 200 ms
QUIC resumed with 0-RTT 0 RTT 1 RTT 100 ms

Add one more RTT to the QUIC rows if the server sends Retry, and one more if the server's first flight exceeds the amplification limit.

The amplification budget. A padded client Initial of 1,200 bytes gives the server 3 × 1,200 = 3,600 bytes before validation. Roughly what the server must fit (sizes vary): ServerHello about 90–130 bytes, EncryptedExtensions about 50–150 (transport parameters, ALPN), CertificateVerify about 80 bytes (ECDSA P-256) or 260 (RSA-2048), Finished 36, plus about 40–60 bytes per packet of QUIC header, packet number and AEAD tag. That leaves roughly 3,100–3,200 bytes for the certificate chain. A leaf and one intermediate with ECDSA keys typically fit. An RSA chain with a long intermediate, or extra unneeded certificates, may not. TLS certificate compression (RFC 8879) is the main lever. Post-quantum signatures, when they arrive in certificates, will make this budget much tighter.

Initial congestion window (RFC 9002 §7.2): min(10 × max_datagram_size, max(14,720, 2 × max_datagram_size)). At a 1,200-byte datagram size: min(12,000, max(14,720, 2,400)) = 12,000 bytes. At 1,472 (a full IPv4 1,500-byte MTU): min(14,720, max(14,720, 2,944)) = 14,720 bytes. The minimum window after a loss is 2 × max_datagram_size.

Initial PTO before any RTT sample (RFC 9002 §6.2.2): the initial RTT is 333 ms, so smoothed_rtt = 333 and rttvar = 333 / 2 = 166.5. In the Initial and Handshake spaces, max_ack_delay counts as 0. PTO = 333 + 4 × 166.5 = 999 ms. Each consecutive PTO without progress doubles it: about 1 s, 2 s, 4 s. A lost first Initial therefore costs a full second, the same ballpark as TCP's 1 s initial RTO (RFC 6298) for a lost SYN.

Loss detection at 40 ms RTT. Time threshold = 9/8 × 40 = 45 ms after sending, if a later packet has been acknowledged. Packet threshold = 3 packets. The timer granularity floor (kGranularity) is 1 ms.

ACK pacing. The default max_ack_delay transport parameter is 25 ms. Receivers should acknowledge at least every second ack-eliciting packet. ACKs themselves are not ack-eliciting, so two idle peers don't ACK each other's ACKs forever.

Per-packet overhead in steady state, IPv4, 8-byte CIDs, 2-byte packet numbers:

Part Bytes
IPv4 + UDP 20 + 8 = 28
Short header: first byte + DCID + packet number 1 + 8 + 2 = 11
AEAD tag 16
STREAM frame header (type, stream ID, offset, length) about 4–8
Total about 59–63

On a 1,500-byte MTU that leaves about 1,437–1,441 bytes of application data per packet. TCP with timestamps plus TLS records is in the same range; the overheads are comparable, the CPU costs are not.

Limits you will meet. CIDs: 0–20 bytes in v1. active_connection_id_limit default 2 (the minimum). Stateless reset token: 16 bytes, and a stateless reset packet is at least 21 bytes so it can pass as a short-header packet. Stream IDs and packet numbers: 62 bits. Varint maximum: 2^62 − 1.

NAT timeouts versus idle timeouts. RFC 4787 recommends NATs keep UDP mappings at least 2 minutes, but many home routers and carrier NATs use 30–60 seconds, and some less. QUIC's max_idle_timeout is often 30 seconds or more. If the NAT forgets the mapping first, server-to-client packets are dropped until the client sends something. Implementations that want to hold an idle connection open send a PING every 15–25 seconds. These are typical values that vary by device and implementation; measure your own clients.

CPU. Early large deployments reported QUIC using roughly two to three times the server CPU of TCP+TLS for the same bytes. GSO, GRO, sendmmsg, batching and better crypto paths have closed much of that gap, but not all of it. Whether HTTP/3 costs you 5% or 50% more CPU depends on the stack, the kernel, the NIC, and the traffic mix. Benchmark your own.

Deep dive: production failure modes

"We enabled HTTP/3 and nobody uses it"

Symptom. Server config says listen 443 quic, but dashboards show 0% h3.

Mechanism. Usually one of three. No UDP listener reachable: the security group, firewall, or load balancer only allows TCP 443. Or no discovery: the Alt-Svc header is missing because a proxy or CDN in front strips it, or it advertises the wrong port. Or clients try, fail, and mark h3 broken, so they stay on TCP.

Prove it.

$ curl -sI https://example.com/ | grep -i alt-svc          # is it advertised?
alt-svc: h3=":443"; ma=86400
$ curl -sv --http3-only -o /dev/null https://example.com/   # can QUIC actually connect?
*   Trying 198.51.100.7:443...
* QUIC connect to 198.51.100.7 port 443 failed: Timeout was reached

The exact error text depends on the curl build; the meaning is the same. Then check the path: from a host outside the network, capture udp port 443 on the server (sudo tcpdump -ni any udp port 443). Initials arriving with no reply means the server isn't listening or isn't bound to that address. Nothing arriving means a filter upstream drops UDP.

Fix. Open UDP 443 at every hop. Make sure the load balancer has a UDP listener. Emit Alt-Svc from whatever actually faces clients. Publish an HTTPS record with alpn="h3,h2" if your DNS supports it.

Connections die when phones change networks — only behind the new load balancer

Symptom. Mobile clients see periodic request failures and reconnects. The old setup (one server) never did this. The new setup (L4 load balancer, many backends) does.

Mechanism. The load balancer hashes the 4-tuple. A NAT rebinding or a network switch gives the client a new address and port. The same DCID now hashes to a different backend, which has never heard of that connection. That backend either drops the packet (the client times out) or, if it can, sends a stateless reset: a packet that looks like a short-header packet and ends in the 16-byte reset token tied to that CID. The client sees the reset and tears the connection down.

Prove it. Server logs or qlogs show stateless_reset sent, or unknown-CID packets dropped. Client qlogs show the connection closed by a stateless reset right after a path change. Correlate with client address changes in the access logs.

Fix. Route on the connection ID. The server encodes a backend identifier in every CID it issues — in the clear, or encrypted with a key shared with the load balancer — and the load balancer reads the DCID from each packet to pick the backend. The IETF QUIC-LB draft standardises this; several load balancers implement their own variants. Within one host, the same problem exists between worker processes. nginx's quic_bpf on;, for example, installs an eBPF program that steers each packet to the worker owning its CID. Short headers carry no CID length, so the load balancer must be told the length the servers use. If you can't route by CID, send disable_active_migration and accept that NAT rebinding still breaks connections.

The handshake takes two round trips, but only for some sites

Symptom. QUIC time-to-first-byte is one RTT worse than expected for one hostname, normal for others on the same servers.

Mechanism. That hostname's certificate chain doesn't fit in 3,600 bytes. The server sends what it can, then waits: it may not send more until the client's next packet arrives and raises the budget.

Prove it. In the server's qlog, the server stops sending after about 3,600 bytes with data still queued; some stacks log an explicit "amplification limited" state. In a pcap, the server's first flight totals about 3,600 bytes of UDP payload, then pauses one RTT until the client's next datagram. Check the chain size: openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com -showcerts </dev/null 2>/dev/null | openssl crl2pkcs7 -nocrl -certfile /dev/stdin | openssl pkcs7 -outform DER | wc -c gives a close approximation of the DER chain size.

Fix. Serve only the leaf and the necessary intermediates (never the root). Prefer ECDSA certificates. Enable certificate compression if your stack supports it.

Throughput on HTTP/3 is worse than on HTTP/2

Symptom. Large downloads are slower over h3 than h2 from the same server. CPU on the server is higher.

Mechanism. Usually the kernel dropping datagrams that the process didn't read fast enough (small socket buffers), which QUIC treats as congestion loss. Sometimes CPU saturation in the QUIC stack itself, with no GSO and one syscall per packet.

Prove it. nstat -az | grep UdpRcvbufErrors rising during the transfer on Linux; netstat -s -p udp "dropped due to full socket buffers" on macOS. The quic-go warning shown earlier. In a qlog, losses with no matching drop on the network path. perf top showing time in crypto and sendmsg.

Fix. Raise net.core.rmem_max/wmem_max and make sure the application requests large buffers. Enable GSO/GRO in the stack. Scale out workers with SO_REUSEPORT plus CID-aware steering.

Duplicate side effects from 0-RTT

Symptom. Rare duplicated actions — two password-reset emails, a doubled counter — clustered around client reconnects.

Mechanism. 0-RTT is enabled at the edge and forwards early data to origins that assume each request arrives once. A replayed or retransmitted early-data flight executes twice.

Prove it. Edge logs that mark early data (many proxies can log whether a request arrived in 0-RTT), joined with the duplicated requests. Look for Early-Data: 1 at the origin.

Fix. Allow 0-RTT only for safe methods, have the edge answer 425 Too Early for anything else, and make non-idempotent endpoints use idempotency keys regardless.

Long-lived idle connections silently stop receiving

Symptom. A client holding an idle HTTP/3 connection (a dashboard, a push-like stream) stops getting server messages after about 30–60 seconds of silence. Sending a request "wakes it up".

Mechanism. The client's NAT dropped the UDP mapping. Server packets to the old external port are dropped. When the client next sends, the NAT creates a new mapping, the server sees the known DCID from a new port, validates the path, and the connection continues — QUIC survived the rebinding, but anything the server sent in between was lost and has to be recovered.

Prove it. Client and server qlogs show a path change (new peer port) right after an idle gap. A pcap on the client's LAN side shows no inbound traffic during the gap.

Fix. Keepalive PING frames more often than the shortest NAT timeout you need to support, typically every 15–25 seconds, for connections that must stay reachable. Keep max_idle_timeout and the keepalive interval consistent with each other.

Handshake fails with a crypto error

Symptom. curl --http3-only fails immediately; the server logs a CONNECTION_CLOSE with error 0x178.

Mechanism. QUIC maps TLS alerts to transport error codes as 0x100 + alert number. 0x178 = 0x100 + 120 = no_application_protocol: the ALPN didn't match. The client offered h3; the server only accepted something else (a test server configured for hq-interop, or a draft version token). Other common ones: 0x128 (alert 40, handshake_failure), 0x12a (alert 42, bad_certificate).

Prove it. qlog connection_closed event, or curl -v showing the close reason.

Fix. Align the ALPN lists. In 2026 the only token you want is h3; draft tokens like h3-29 are long obsolete.

Deep dive: 2026 context

Where QUIC actually runs. In most deployments QUIC is a last-mile protocol. Browsers and mobile apps speak HTTP/3 to a CDN or cloud edge. The edge speaks HTTP/1.1 or HTTP/2 over TCP to the origin, inside a data centre or over a private backbone where loss is rare and HTTP/3's advantages are small. Major CDNs carry a large share of their request volume as HTTP/3. Origins and service meshes mostly don't use it yet.

Cloud load balancers and Kubernetes. The question to ask any load balancer is: does it terminate QUIC (it runs the QUIC stack and you get HTTP on the inside), pass through UDP routed by connection ID, pass through UDP hashed by 4-tuple (which breaks migration), or not support UDP 443 at all? Answers differ by provider and product and change frequently, so check current documentation. In Kubernetes, a Service of type LoadBalancer exposing both TCP 443 and UDP 443 needs mixed-protocol support, which has been stable since v1.26 but must also be supported by your cloud's load-balancer controller. Ingress controllers and gateways (Envoy-based ones, nginx, HAProxy and others) can terminate HTTP/3, each with its own maturity and configuration.

MASQUE: QUIC as a tunnel. HTTP/3 can carry UDP (CONNECT-UDP, RFC 9298) and IP packets (CONNECT-IP, RFC 9484), using HTTP Datagrams (RFC 9297) over QUIC DATAGRAM frames (RFC 9221). This is how several privacy relays and some zero-trust access clients now build their tunnels: the traffic looks like ordinary HTTP/3 to port 443. For network teams, it means "block UDP 443" increasingly blocks VPN-like products as well as web traffic.

WebTransport gives browsers bidirectional streams and datagrams over HTTP/3, the successor to WebSockets for latency-sensitive apps. It depends on the extended CONNECT mechanism (RFC 9220).

Post-quantum key exchange. Browsers and major servers now negotiate hybrid key exchange (X25519 combined with ML-KEM-768). An ML-KEM-768 public key is 1,184 bytes, so the ClientHello no longer fits in one 1,200-byte Initial packet; it spans two. QUIC handles that cleanly — CRYPTO frames have offsets — but it is one reason you will see two client Initial datagrams in 2026 captures where older captures had one. It also means the client sends 2,400 bytes or more before the server replies, so the server's amplification budget grows to 7,200 bytes or more.

Versions and greasing. QUIC v2 (RFC 9369) is functionally identical to v1 but uses different constants (a new version number, salt, and packet-type encoding). Its main purpose is to keep middleboxes from hard-coding v1. Compatible version negotiation (RFC 9368) lets a connection upgrade versions without an extra round trip. RFC 9287 lets endpoints randomise the "fixed" bit. All of it is the same anti-ossification strategy: exercise every degree of freedom so nobody can assume it's constant.

Visibility for operators. RFC 9312 (QUIC manageability) is the honest guide to what network operators can and cannot observe. The spin bit is the main deliberate concession: an optional bit that flips once per RTT, so an on-path observer can estimate RTT. Implementations may disable it, and many do on some or all connections. Expect to measure QUIC performance from endpoint telemetry — qlog, server metrics, client RUM — not from taps in the middle.

In-kernel QUIC. Work to add a QUIC implementation to the Linux kernel has been under way, aiming at in-kernel users (such as SMB over QUIC) and hardware offload. Treat its availability as version-specific; most production QUIC in 2026 still runs in user space.

Deep dive: senior interview questions

Q1: QUIC encrypts its packets. So why can Wireshark decode the ClientHello without any keys?

A: Initial packets are protected with keys derived from the client's first Destination Connection ID and a public salt published in RFC 9001. The DCID is in cleartext, so anyone can derive the keys. Initial protection defends against off-path injection and casual middlebox parsing, not against an on-path observer. Real secrecy starts with Handshake keys, derived from the (EC)DHE exchange, and the certificate travels under those.

Q2: QUIC retransmits frames, not packets. What does that buy?

A: Every transmission gets a new, monotonically increasing packet number, so an ACK always identifies exactly which transmission arrived. RTT samples are never ambiguous, unlike TCP's retransmissions (Karn's problem). It also lets the sender drop stale information — an old ACK or a superseded MAX_DATA — instead of resending it, and repack lost data with other streams' data.

Q3: Why does QUIC have three packet number spaces instead of one?

A: Each encryption level's packets are protected with different keys, and they're discarded at different times. If one space spanned levels, an ACK for an Initial packet could arrive in a 1-RTT packet, or packet numbers would have gaps as keys were dropped. Separate spaces keep acknowledgement, loss detection and key discard per level clean. 0-RTT and 1-RTT share the Application space because they carry the same kind of data.

Q4: Your L4 load balancer hashes on the 5-tuple. What breaks with QUIC, and how do you fix it without giving up migration?

A: Any client address change — NAT rebinding or a real network switch — rehashes the connection to a backend with no state for it, which drops the packets or sends a stateless reset. The fix is routing by connection ID: servers encode a routable backend identifier into the CIDs they issue (plain or encrypted, as in the QUIC-LB draft), and the balancer extracts it from the DCID. Since short headers carry no CID length, the length must be agreed in configuration. The same steering is needed between worker processes on one host.

Q5: Why couldn't HTTP/3 reuse HPACK?

A: HPACK's dynamic table is updated inline by header blocks and assumes they are processed in the order sent. QUIC streams are independent and arrive in any order, so a header block could reference an entry whose insertion is on a stream that hasn't arrived — head-of-line blocking reintroduced by the compressor, or outright corruption. QPACK moves insertions to a dedicated encoder stream, makes each header block declare the insert count it needs, caps the number of streams allowed to block, and acknowledges via a decoder stream so the encoder can choose references that never block.

Q6: What exactly limits how much a QUIC server sends before it knows the client's address is real?

A: The anti-amplification limit: three times the bytes received from that address, until the address is validated. Validation happens when the server successfully processes a Handshake packet from the client, when the client presents a valid Retry or NEW_TOKEN token, or through path validation. That's why client Initials are padded to 1,200 bytes, and why oversized certificate chains add a round trip.

Q7: A PTO fires. How is that different from a TCP RTO?

A: A PTO means "I haven't heard anything for longer than expected", not "packets are definitely lost". The sender sends one or two probe packets — new data if it has any, otherwise retransmitted data or a PING — to elicit an ACK, and backs off exponentially on repeated PTOs. It doesn't collapse the congestion window. Packets are only declared lost by the ACK-based thresholds, and congestion response follows real loss (with persistent congestion handling for long blackouts). A classic TCP RTO resets cwnd to one segment.

Q8: How would you prove that an HTTP/3 performance problem is in the host rather than the network?

A: Compare what the endpoint thinks happened with what the network did. In the qlog, find the losses and their triggers. On the same host, check kernel UDP drop counters (UdpRcvbufErrors with nstat, or netstat -s -p udp on macOS) over the same interval. If the kernel dropped datagrams that QUIC then counted as lost, the problem is socket buffers or a process that isn't reading fast enough. Confirm with CPU profiles of the QUIC worker. A capture on the host's NIC showing the packets arrived settles it.

Q9: When would you deliberately not enable HTTP/3?

A: Internal east-west traffic in a low-loss data centre, where it adds CPU and operational complexity for little gain. Paths where you can't route UDP by connection ID and clients change addresses often — you'd get the cost without migration. Environments where UDP 443 is filtered for most clients. And any deployment where you can't keep TCP as a fallback. Wherever you do enable it, measure h3 against h2 per client network before declaring a win.
